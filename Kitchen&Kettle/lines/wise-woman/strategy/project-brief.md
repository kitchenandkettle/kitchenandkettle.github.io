# Project Brief

## What this is

A modern wise woman bringing useful old-world knowledge back into ordinary modern life.

The project asks what people once did to stay nourished, steady, warm, cared for, and connected to the seasons — then finds the simple versions that still fit a person who has to rush out of the house for work. It is about recovering small practices that time and modern schedules have pushed aside, without requiring five hours of preparation or a complete change of lifestyle.

The project draws especially from Old Norse culture and worldview, but is not a Viking-only account. It can include traditional knowledge, folk medicine, herbs, foodways, preservation, seasonal living, nature observation, mythology, spirituality, household skills, animals, farming, and craft. The focus is not simply on making old-fashioned things. It is on how knowledge was understood, practiced, recorded, and carried through time.

## What it should feel like

Earthy. Intelligent. Curious. Cozy. Slightly mysterious. Historically grounded. Practical. Feminine without being overly girly. Spiritual without being preachy. Rustic without being performative.

## What it refuses to be

- Viking nostalgia or “the past did everything better”
- Pseudo-history presented as fact
- Empty witchy aesthetics
- Conspiracy content
- Fear-based wellness
- Anti-modern-medicine messaging
- Unsupported medical claims
- A performance of expertise

## Editorial standard

Every historical or health-related claim gets a confidence label:

1. Historically documented
2. Reasonable scholarly reconstruction
3. Later folklore or uncertain attribution
4. Modern reconstruction
5. My own modern practice

Historical medicinal use is not the same as modern clinical evidence. Safety, interactions, contraindications, and uncertainty stay visible.

## Old Norse layer

Old Norse words, phrases, gods, stories, and associations can connect the project to its particular historical interest. They should appear only where they genuinely fit the subject and can be supported by accurate research. Nothing gets a Norse reference just to make it feel older, moodier, or more distinctive.

When a connection is uncertain, later, reconstructed, or personal rather than documented, label it plainly. The project can include a modern interpretation, but it must not present that interpretation as surviving Old Norse fact.

## Content pillars to test

- The Medicine Woman — herbs, remedies, preparations, and historical healing
- The Kitchen — foodways, cooking, fermentation, and preservation
- The Wild — plants, animals, farming, and nature observation
- The Old Ways — practical household skills and traditional crafts
- The Norse World — language, mythology, worldview, history, and material culture
- The Modern Wise Woman — thoughtful applications in contemporary life

## Audience promise

Come learn beside me. We will look closely, distinguish what we know from what we are guessing, and bring back small forms of comfort and nourishment that can fit into a modern morning, evening, or workday.

The emotional promise is: old knowledge that helps cold bones get through an early winter morning. One simple thing can make a rushed day feel more cared for.

## Positioning distinction

Kitchen & Kettle is practical modern homekeeping: what works now. Wise Woman is the annotated old ways: what is documented, what is uncertain, what can be reconstructed, and what still has meaning or use.

The audience is inclusive of both genders. “Wise Woman” describes the founder’s role as the medicine woman and guide, not the audience’s identity.

## Working product direction

- The Old Ways Almanac — main seasonal guide
- Hearth & Hedgerow Cards — annotated lore cards
- Lore Papers — focused investigations into one question
- Field Archive Notebook — personal record of the living world and household knowledge
- Apothecary and specimen record sheets — companion downloads
- One combined physical Field Archive / Apothecary Kit

Fermentation, dyes, herbal preparations, and other practical subjects belong inside relevant Almanac editions or Lore Papers. They do not define the brand on their own.
