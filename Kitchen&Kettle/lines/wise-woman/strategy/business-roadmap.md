# Wise Woman Business Roadmap

## Working direction

Old-world knowledge for modern mornings, evenings, and workdays.

This project brings back small forms of comfort, nourishment, and steadiness that people have used for centuries but that modern schedules have pushed aside. The audience may work full-time, live in a town or city, rent their home, and have no interest in becoming a homesteader. They want something that helps cold bones get through an early winter morning without five hours of preparation or a complete lifestyle overhaul.

This is a working roadmap, not a commitment to build every offer listed here. Each stage earns the next one.

## The customer promise

For people who want an older, slower, more connected way of living but have modern constraints, Wise Woman provides carefully researched practices and realistic ways to bring comfort and nourishment into ordinary days.

Every piece should answer:

- What did people historically do?
- What do we actually know about it?
- What is uncertain, reconstructed, or modern?
- What might have made the practice useful?
- How can someone try a small version after work?
- What safety limits or medical boundaries matter?

The useful result is not historical cosplay or a promise to heal every problem. It is one small, believable practice that helps a modern day feel more cared for.

## How this differs from Kitchen & Kettle

Kitchen & Kettle owns practical modern homekeeping: what works now in the kitchen, household, homestead, and body care.

Wise Woman owns the translation layer: where an old practice came from, how people understood it, what evidence remains, and how a small part of it might fit modern life.

A topic can overlap. The question must not.

- Kitchen & Kettle: How do I make and use this at home?
- Wise Woman: What did people do, what did it mean, what can we verify, and what can I carry forward now?

If an offer could move to Kitchen & Kettle without changing its customer promise, it does not belong here yet.

## Offer architecture

The business should have four layers. Do not launch all four at once.

### 1. Free editorial content: discovery and trust

Short articles, videos, emails, or visual posts built around one old practice and one modern application.

Recurring format:

- The old practice
- The evidence and uncertainty
- The modern translation
- The workday version
- Safety and limits

Examples:

- Broth, porridges, and nourishing drinks
- Historical mushroom use versus modern mushroom coffee claims
- Warming baths and foot soaks
- Evening darkness, quiet, and sleep practices
- Seasonal eating for people who do not grow their own food
- Herb preservation for a small kitchen
- Nature observation for people who live in cities

Purpose: learn what people respond to before making products.

### 2. Small digital offers: accessible first purchase

Short, focused downloads priced and scoped as useful papers rather than huge courses.

Working format: Lore Papers.

Each paper covers one question and includes:

- A readable investigation
- Source notes
- Confidence labels where relevant
- One or two modern practices
- A realistic ten-minute version
- Safety notes and a short record page

Possible titles:

- The Workday Apothecary: What Belongs in a Small Home Cabinet
- The Evening Hearth: Older Ways of Making Night Feel Like Night
- Broth, Porridge, and the Warm Cup: Old Nourishment for Modern Days
- The City Forager's Attention: Learning Plants Without Pretending to Be an Herbalist
- What to Keep for Winter: Small Acts of Seasonal Preparation

These should be tested one at a time. Do not build a library before learning which subjects people want.

### 3. Flagship digital offer: a seasonal system

Working title: The Old Ways Almanac.

This is the main digital product once several smaller pieces show a clear pattern of interest. It is not a generic planner or encyclopedia. It is a seasonal guide to practices worth carrying forward.

Possible structure:

- One seasonal theme
- A handful of researched old practices
- What is documented versus reconstructed
- Modern versions for busy households
- Food, rest, body, nature, and household sections
- A small seasonal practice list
- Record pages for what the customer actually tried
- Sources and safety notes

The Almanac should be released as a focused edition first, not an endless annual obligation. A seasonal release can be evaluated before committing to a year-round publishing schedule.

### 4. Physical tools: practice, not remedies

Physical products should help customers observe, record, prepare, or continue a practice. They should not be the business's first proof of concept.

Best candidates:

- Field Archive Notebook
- Hearth & Hedgerow Cards
- Apothecary labels and herb tags
- Preparation and observation record sheets
- Specimen envelopes or gathering tags
- One combined Field Archive / Apothecary Kit

The kit should contain tools, not ingestible herbs, tinctures, salves, oils, or anything sold as a treatment.

Physical products come only after a digital subject has proven interest and one prototype remains manageable after the third unit.

## Roadmap by stage

### Stage 0: clarify and test the idea

Goal: find out whether the subject is enjoyable to make and recognizable to other people.

Make three short pieces:

1. One old-world drink
2. One body-care or rest practice
3. One seasonal or household practice

Each must include a modern workday version.

Success signal:

- One subject clearly holds attention
- The work feels distinct from Kitchen & Kettle
- People respond with recognition, questions, saves, shares, or requests for more

Do not make a product yet.

### Stage 1: build a small editorial shelf

Goal: identify the first real content pillar.

Make 5–8 pieces around the strongest subject from Stage 0. Keep the format consistent so the audience can understand what Wise Woman does.

Possible recurring series names:

- Old Ways, Workday Version
- The Modern Hearth
- A Practice Worth Carrying
- Field Notes for Ordinary Days

Do not commit to the final series name yet.

Success signal:

- One recurring question appears in audience responses
- One topic gets stronger engagement than the others
- The audience can describe the project in one sentence

### Stage 2: release the first Lore Paper

Goal: turn the strongest editorial subject into one small paid download.

The first paid paper should be narrow, useful, and finishable. It may contain:

- 10–20 pages
- Research and source notes
- One or two practical modern adaptations
- A workday version
- A short journal or tracking section

No course, membership, subscription, or large bundle yet.

Success signal:

- People buy without needing a large catalog
- Buyers understand what they are getting
- The subject generates a natural follow-up

### Stage 3: create a small collection

Goal: establish a coherent first line without creating inventory pressure.

Offer:

- 2–3 Lore Papers
- One related record book or notebook
- Optional bundle pricing

The papers should connect through one clear question, such as nourishment, seasonal living, or household care. Do not mix every possible Wise Woman subject into the first collection.

Success signal:

- One paper is a clear entry point
- One companion product genuinely helps people use the ideas
- The collection feels like a body of work rather than a random Etsy catalog

### Stage 4: make the first Old Ways Almanac edition

Goal: create the flagship offer from subjects that already proved useful.

The Almanac should gather the strongest material into a seasonal system. It should not simply repackage the Lore Papers. It needs a larger organizing structure and a clear reason to buy the complete edition.

Possible components:

- Seasonal research notes
- Modern practice menu
- Workday and weekend versions
- Source and confidence notes
- Simple record pages
- A seasonal closing or reflection section

Release one edition. Review the workload before promising another.

### Stage 5: prototype one physical kit

Goal: add a tactile product without creating a stock-heavy herbal business.

Prototype:

- One notebook
- Herb labels and hanging tags
- Preparation and observation cards
- Specimen envelopes
- A short instruction card

Make one prototype, then three identical units. Record sourcing time, assembly time, storage space, packaging, weight, and what feels like fulfillment work.

Only consider a small release if the third unit still feels manageable.

## What the business should not offer at launch

Do not begin with:

- Loose tea blends
- Tinctures
- Salves or balms
- Infused oils
- Homemade candles
- Large ingredient collections
- Anything marketed as a treatment or cure
- One-on-one health advice
- A subscription box
- A daily posting schedule
- A large physical catalog
- A broad herbalism course

These add inventory, safety, regulatory, customer-support, or medical-claim pressure before the project has proven demand.

## The simplest possible first business

If the project eventually becomes a business, the first viable version is:

- A clear editorial series about old-world practices for modern schedules
- One focused Lore Paper
- One related record or practice download
- Occasional seasonal editions when the workload allows

Physical products are optional. They are not required for the idea to be real.

## Decision rules

Before adding an offer, ask:

1. Does this serve the old-practice-to-modern-life promise?
2. Is it meaningfully different from Kitchen & Kettle?
3. Does it answer a real audience desire rather than only express an interesting topic?
4. Can it be made after a full workday?
5. Does it avoid unnecessary medical claims and inventory risk?
6. Has a smaller version already shown interest?

If the answer is no to several of these, keep it as a research or content idea instead of turning it into a product.

## Current next step

Begin with a fall three-piece test batch. Research five fall practices, choose three, and make one short piece for each. Watch which subject pulls people in. Winter Mornings remains the next seasonal edition after the fall test.

The first product decision comes after that evidence. Not before.
