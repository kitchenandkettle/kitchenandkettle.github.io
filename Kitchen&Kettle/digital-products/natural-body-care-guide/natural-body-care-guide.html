Natural Body
Care Guide

Simple ingredients. Honest tradeoffs.

My natural routine for hair, face, and body. What works for me, what did not work for my husband, and how to tell which camp your skin is in.

A companion to My Simple Body Care Guide. My husband follows the fragrance-free routine in that guide. I follow the natural routine in this one.

Same house, two skins.

Simple body care works best for my husband. Natural body care works best for me. Both of us are right, because we have different skin.

My husband spent years reacting to natural essential-oil soaps before we connected it. His skin calmed down when we went free and clear, with short ingredient lists and no fragrance. Coconut oil plus Oregon grape infusion calms his skin. Castile is harsh on my hands. That story is in the Simple guide, and it still stands.

My skin went the other way. Fewer lab products and more kitchen staples left my hair lighter, my scalp calmer, and my routine cheaper. I keep it plain, I keep the ratios weak, and I stop anything that irritates.

Honest note

This guide is not a replacement for the Simple guide. If your skin reacts to fragrance, essential oils, or baking soda, use that guide instead. Natural does not mean gentle. It means you are the one watching for reactions.

How to use this guide

Start with hair, because that is the most specific recipe here. Then try one body change at a time. Give each change two weeks. If your skin is happy, keep it. If it is not, go back to what worked.

Notes:

My Hair Wash — Baking Soda + Vinegar

This is my exact method. No exact lab measurement, just my bathroom cup and a small pinch.

Wash cup

  • About 1/2 tsp baking soda, a small pinch
  • Warm water to the top of a small bathroom drinking cup, roughly 3 to 4 oz. Stir until fully clear.
  • Cold water will not dissolve it properly. Use warm.

Rinse cup

  • A small splash of apple cider vinegar, about 1 to 2 tsp, in a second cup
  • Warm water to the top, stir
  • 1 to 2 drops pine essential oil, if your skin can tolerate it. Patch test first.

Steps

  1. Wet hair fully with warm water.
  2. Pour the wash cup slowly over scalp. Massage with fingertips 30 to 60 seconds. Do not scrub lengths.
  3. Rinse well with warm water.
  4. Pour the rinse cup over scalp and hair. Leave about 30 seconds.
  5. Rinse lightly. A quick rinse leaves softness without vinegar smell.

Honest note

If your hair feels heavy or slicked down after, you are likely using too much baking soda or too much vinegar. I tend to go big or go home, and I had to cut both way back before this worked for me. Less is more here.

My notes on this recipe:

Hair Wash (continued)

Frequency

Start once a week. Most hair does not need this more than twice a week. If scalp feels tight or hair feels straw-like, space it out, use less baking soda, or go back to a gentle shampoo for a while.

Honest note

Baking soda is alkaline, about pH 9. Hair and scalp prefer slightly acidic. This works for me because I keep it very dilute and I always follow with the vinegar rinse. Used strong, used often, or left on too long, it can dry and break hair. This is not for color-treated hair. Stop if you get irritation, itching, or extra shedding.

Troubleshooting

  • Waxy or coated feeling: Wash did not dissolve fully, or did not rinse enough. Use warmer water, stir longer, rinse longer.
  • Dry ends: Keep wash on scalp only. Let rinse run through ends, do not scrub them.
  • Vinegar smell lingers: You used too much vinegar. Drop to 1 tsp. Rinse a little longer.
  • Essential oil itch: Skip the oil next time. Essential oils are a potential skin irritant, even in tiny amounts.

Notes:

Between Washes

Grease, sweat, and second-day hair live here.

Grease

My roots get greasy fast. If yours do too, you will be tempted to wash every single day. Try this first.

A lot of people use a boar bristle brush to pull grease from the roots down to the ends. I tried it and it never moved the grease for me. My roots just held onto it, and I started doubting the whole routine.

What worked was a silicone brush. It actually moves the grease along. Use it on dry hair between washes, or in the shower if you are just rinsing with water. It helps stimulate the scalp too.

Sweaty days

Yard work the day after wash day is my whole summer. Here is the thing I had to learn: sweat is mostly water and salt, not oil, so it rarely needs the full wash. Start with a warm water rinse, silicone brush on the scalp while you are in there, and always finish with the vinegar cup, since sweat smell means bacteria and vinegar handles that better than water.

If hair still looks gross the next morning, shake a little arrowroot powder at the roots, wait two minutes, and brush it out. It drinks up the leftover sweat overnight. We use it in a future recipe too, so it is worth having around.

Still dirty after that, do a half strength scalp wash: quarter teaspoon instead of half, scalp only, then the vinegar cup. Stronger than water, weaker than wash day. In peak summer, two weak washes a week beats one strong wash plus rinses that do not work. Raise frequency, lower strength.

Notes:

Face

Warm water most days, honey or Ivory when needed. Sunscreen every morning, jojoba plus actives at night, tallow to seal it in.

Morning

Warm water and a clean washcloth is often enough. If face feels oily, a thin raw honey wash: pea-sized honey on damp skin, massage 30 seconds, rinse warm, or a really simple soap like Ivory bar soap.

The only moisturizer you need in the morning is a simple broad spectrum sunscreen. Use a tallow-based sunscreen to keep it natural, or something like CeraVe sunscreen if you want a little anti-aging benefit from niacinamide. Either way, use sunscreen every morning.

Evening

Remove sunscreen and grime first. Jojoba oil is close to skin's own sebum, so it sinks in light, softens without feeling greasy, and seals in dampness from washing.

For anti-aging in the evening: apply vitamin C serum to clean skin first, then a thin layer of jojoba oil, then retinol serum. Start retinol two nights a week and build up. If actives sting, buffer with jojoba first.

Bed time

Seal it all in with a thin layer of tallow balm. It is the best overnight occlusive that also nourishes. Tallow is close to skin's own oils, so it holds moisture in while it softens. Apply last, over serums and jojoba, and let it work overnight.

Honest note

I picked jojoba and tallow because they match what skin already makes. Jojoba acts like sebum, so it softens without feeling heavy. Tallow acts like skin's own fats, so it seals moisture in overnight and feeds it at the same time. One lightens, one seals. That pair covers both jobs.

Notes:

Body

One bar for washing, damp skin for moisture, my deodorant mix when I have time and Schmidt's when I do not.

Wash

Use one mild bar for body and hands. Soap only where you need it: underarms, feet, and private areas. Warm water and a washcloth is enough everywhere else. More soap just dries skin out.

On natural versus store bought: every true soap runs alkaline, around pH 9 to 10, while skin sits near 5.5, so all of them fight skin a little. What matters is how much oil survives the lye. Castile converts every drop of its olive oil into soap and leaves nothing behind to soften the bar, which is why pure olive oil soap strips harder than it sounds like it should. Bar or liquid, same story, and the liquid needs heavy diluting before it touches skin. I do not even use it for dishes since I do not want it on my hands. I pour a bit into the laundry, and that is it.

A superfatted bar is the gentlest natural option. That word just means the maker held back extra oil on purpose instead of letting the lye convert it all, so free oil stays in the finished bar and cushions your skin. When you read a label, look for bars heavy on tallow, olive, or shea. Go light on coconut oil, which cleans strongly and strips skin hardest.

How to tell them apart on a label: if it says castile, or 100 percent olive oil, it is castile. A superfatted bar names extra oils beyond the soap itself, look for plain oil words like olive oil or shea butter listed after the saponified oils, or the word superfatted on the wrapper. Buying from a maker, just ask what the superfat is. A maker who knows the number is a maker you can trust.

Skip fragrance every time, essential oils included. Scent, natural or not, is the most common irritant in soap, so fragrance free is the safest shelf to shop. Ivory sits in the middle for me, a simple true soap with no scent, sold everywhere, cheap.

My notes on body:

Moisture

Every shower ends here. Damp skin, then seal it in.

Do not towel fully dry. Pat lightly and leave skin damp, then moisturize right away. Use tallow balm to seal moisture in, jojoba oil on lighter days, or plain shea. The dampness is what you are sealing in.

Straight shea can feel heavy. To lighten it, whip it: soften 1/2 cup shea at room temp, beat with a hand mixer 3 to 5 minutes until fluffy, then beat in 2 Tbsp jojoba oil and 1 tsp arrowroot powder. The arrowroot cuts the greasy feel. Store in a jar at room temp.

Notes:

Deodorant

My mix in a jar, Schmidt's when life gets busy.

My mix:

  • 1 cup shea butter
  • 1/2 cup coconut oil
  • 1 Tbsp beeswax
  • 3 cups arrowroot powder
  • 1 Tbsp plus 1 tsp baking soda
  • 15 drops each sage and lavender essential oils, if your skin can tolerate them

Melt shea, coconut oil, and beeswax gently. Stir in arrowroot and baking soda, then the oils. Pour into jars. Use a pea-sized amount per underarm.

Honest note

If baking soda underarms burn or darken, stop. Switch to arrowroot only, or back to a store deodorant that works. When life gets too busy and I do not have time to whip up my own, I use Schmidt's.

Notes:

Teeth

Homemade paste cleans. Fluoride prevents cavities. I tried going without and bought toothpaste again.

Homemade paste

Usual mix is coconut oil plus baking soda, sometimes a drop of peppermint oil. It scrubs and freshens. It does not remineralize enamel the way fluoride does. Baking soda is mildly abrasive, fine for most people in small amounts, harsh if scrubbed hard every day.

Fluoride

Fluoride has decades of cavity-prevention evidence behind it. It hardens enamel and reverses very early decay. That is why dentists recommend it so strongly. Hydroxyapatite paste is the newer alternative with promising early research, but less long-term data than fluoride. When I was experimenting, hydroxyapatite powder was super expensive, so buying toothpaste was more cost effective.

I tried homemade paste and ended up with horrible tooth pain. Too much baking soda left my teeth severely sensitive.

What I learned digging in: baking soda is not feeding teeth anything, it is just really good at scrubbing. I looked into mixing my own with fluoride, but fluoride is hard to find sold on its own. Everything pointed to buying toothpaste as the most logical option.

Honest note

Buying toothpaste won this one. That is not failure, you do not have to make every little thing. Talk to your dentist before switching, especially for kids.

The Basics

Start here. Everything after this page is extra.

  • Baking soda wash plus vinegar rinse for hair
  • One simple mild soap for body and hands
  • Pat dry, leave skin damp, do not towel fully
  • Jojoba oil for moisture, tallow balm to seal at night
  • Sunscreen every morning
  • Deodorant, mine or Schmidt's

That is the whole routine. Salves, infused oils, bath teas, all of it can wait until you want a project.

Notes:

Bath

Epsom for muscles, baking soda for skin, tea for the mood. No oil, the tub gets slick.

Bath salts: 1 cup Epsom salt plus 1/2 cup baking soda per tub, soak 20 minutes. The Epsom salt is for sore muscles. The baking soda softens the water and soothes dry or itchy skin.

For a nicer soak, add a tea bag to the tub: chamomile, green tea, or a handful of dried rose petals in a muslin bag. They scent the water and leave skin feeling soft.

I do not add oil to my baths. It makes the tub extremely slippery.

Notes:

Infused Oils

Same method as My Simple Body Care Guide. The base for every salve in this guide.

Dried herbs in a jar, covered with oil, left on a sunny windowsill. That is the whole method. The oil pulls the plant compounds out over a few weeks. What you get is a custom oil a store cannot match because you chose the herbs.

What you need: Dried herbs only, fresh herbs mold in oil. Pick calendula for healing, plantain for bites, lavender for calm, chamomile for sensitive skin. A carrier oil, I use jojoba. A clean jar, cheesecloth for straining.

  1. Fill a clean jar about one-third full with dried herb. Do not pack tight.
  2. Pour jojoba over until herbs sit under at least an inch of oil. Cap tight.
  3. Set on a warm windowsill 4 to 6 weeks. Shake when you remember.
  4. Strain through cheesecloth into a dark bottle. Label herb and date. Store cool and dark.

Use straight on damp skin, or save it for salves. Patch test a new herb on your inner arm first, any plant can irritate the wrong person.

Notes:

Salves and Balms

Same method as My Simple Body Care Guide. If you have that guide, you already know this one.

A salve is an infused oil solidified with beeswax so it stays where you put it. Oil drips and spreads. Salve sits on the skin and seals moisture in while the herbs do their work. Beeswax is not just thickener. It makes a breathable barrier that keeps water in and irritants out.

Start with an infused oil: dried calendula, plantain, or lavender steeped in jojoba. That oil is the medicine. The wax just gives it shape.

What you need: 1 cup infused oil, about 1 oz beeswax pastilles, small tins or jars.

  1. Pour oil into a small pot or a heatproof jar set in simmering water. Warm on low.
  2. Stir in beeswax until fully melted. Test firmness with a cold spoon: dip, chill 1 minute, check. Add more wax for firmer, more oil for softer.
  3. Pour into clean tins. Cool fully before lidding or condensation forms inside.
  4. Label with herbs and date. Store cool. No water means no spoilage.

Uses I reach for: calendula for dry hands and scrapes, plantain for bug bites and splinters, lavender as a calming chest rub. One batch of oil becomes several tins.

Honest note

No water means no spoilage. Salves keep for years without preservatives. Label with the herbs and the date.

Notes:

Tallow Balm

Same method as My Simple Body Care Guide. This is the bedtime seal from the Face section.

Tallow is rendered beef fat. It is close to the oils your own skin makes, so it sinks in instead of sitting on top. It carries vitamins A, D, E, and K. Whipped with a little coconut oil it replaces night cream, hand balm, and lip balm in one jar.

What you need: Beef fat trimmings, ask your butcher. A spoonful of coconut oil for spreadability. A clean jar.

  1. Render: cut fat small, crock pot on low or oven at 200F, 4 to 6 hours until liquid. Strain through cheesecloth.
  2. Cool until solid but soft, like room-temp butter.
  3. Whip 2 to 3 minutes until creamy white. Beat in coconut oil while whipping.
  4. Jar it. A little goes a long way, face, hands, elbows, anywhere.

Skip rendering: buy already-rendered tallow at the store, warm to room temp, whip with coconut oil. Or buy tallow-based sunscreen, already rendered with no scent, minerals do the SPF work.

Notes:

Natural Reality Filter

Make what is worth making. Buy what is better from someone with the right equipment.

Make these

  • Hair rinses. Pennies per wash, mixed in a minute. You control the strength.
  • Infused oils. Calendula or plantain in jojoba. Slow, simple, quality you can trace.
  • Salves and balms. Infused oil plus a little beeswax in a tin. Real medicine for hands and dry spots.
  • Bath salts. Epsom plus baking soda. No lab needed.

Buy these

  • Sunscreen. Not a DIY project. Lab-tested broad spectrum SPF 30 is worth every penny.
  • Toothpaste. Fluoride is hard to buy on its own and homemade paste left my teeth sensitive. Buying wins on cost and cavities.
  • Soap if your skin reacts. A milled bar like Ivory or a syndet like Dove Unscented is gentler than a harsh handmade bar. Cardboard packaging, no lye stored in your house.

Honest note

Supporting good makers is not failure. If you love a small-batch soap and your skin loves it back, that is the village model working. Pick the skills you actually want, let good companies handle the rest.

What worked / what did not

A note on natural care

Natural works for me because I watch how my skin responds and I keep everything weak and simple.

It is not better in some moral sense. It is just what my hair and skin like right now. My husband needs free and clear. I need a pinch of soda and a splash of vinegar. Same house, different shelf in the bathroom.

If a recipe makes you feel anxious instead of cared for, simplify it. Good body care should feel like nourishment, not like solving a puzzle.
