How to use this book

Come in and make yourself at home, because this book works the way a good visit does. You read a little and then you do a little, and then you write a little, and that rhythm carries the whole book. Short passages open each part while the practices fit inside an hour, and the lined pages take the rest, so date every entry and watch your year turn in your own hand.

Take safety as part of the welcome and keep it always. Safety first, always. Never ingest yew, mistletoe, or selago in any form. No wild plant identification without an expert beside you — store-bought teas only. Check every herb against your medicines and, if relevant, pregnancy with a clinician. Never leave flame unattended.

Who we mean here, part one

Picture a family of languages rather than a single tribe, scattered from hillforts and salt mines to river towns where traders set up altars. The earliest kin gather around the salt workings at Hallstatt, where timber stairs still stain dark with brine, and later metalworkers at La Tène wind animal shapes into bronze and gold that ripple when you turn them in lamplight. Centuries afterward Gaulish farmers carve short prayers to local gods in stone under Rome, and Irish and Welsh scribes copy old stories into Christian manuscripts by candlelight. Cousins, not twins.

Keep Gaul, Ireland, and Wales as three windows on related but separate houses, and look through one at a time. In the Gaulish window you meet Epona, named on soldiers' dedications from Britain to Bulgaria with almost no story attached — Attested, but spare. In the Welsh window rides Rhiannon on a white horse no rider can catch, from medieval tales — Attested there. In the Irish window stands the Dagda with his club and cauldron, generous and crude in the same hour, from medieval manuscripts too. Kin in speech, distinct in household.

You do not need blood or belief to begin here, only an honest address. Tonight pick one window and stay with it for half an hour at your table: read one short passage, write down which house you visited and one name you greeted, and close the book. Naming the household keeps you courteous. The rest of this book will keep reminding you which door you knocked on.

Who we mean here, part two

This book makes no secret of its limits, and you deserve them plainly before you give it your year. No druid ever wrote down teachings for us to find, and the Roman writers who describe druids watched from the far side of the fire with taxes and wars on their minds, their accounts colored by conquest. Centuries later, Irish and Welsh scribes copied old tales onto vellum in monastery lamplight, saving stories while changing their frames. A broken chain, honestly held.

What you hold instead is a working journal built from what survives, with every claim labeled so you always know the ground under your feet. Where old stones or manuscripts speak, you will hear them as Attested, the firmest ground. Where later folk custom likely carries an older shape, this book names it folk, a fair guess but not proof. Where modern hands fill the gaps with new rites, it says Modern or Reconstructed. You will find the label waiting at the foot of each lore page in its Lore Root, so doubt always has somewhere to sit.

Try this at your table tonight with a pen and ten unhurried minutes. Write one sentence for what you hope this book will give you, and one for what you hope it will leave out, then read both aloud to hear if they sound true. Keep that page marked for the whole year. When a later chapter puzzles or dazzles you, return to those two sentences and let them judge the chapter.

The Three Realms blessing

An old Irish oath runs on three legs, and you can still feel its balance today. In medieval Irish tales the Dagda himself, the good god and father among the Irish gods, swears by Land and Sea and Sky together as his witnesses, staking his word on the whole world holding. Later folk prayers keep naming the same trio over hearth and sickbed, blessing and warding with three. The oath wording is Attested in medieval sources; the wide daily use made of it here is Reconstructed. Three witnesses round one fire.

Treat the three as a plain daily check-in rather than high doctrine. Land is your body and home, everything you can touch and sweep and mend. Sea is the feeling underneath, what moves and dreams and worries in the night. Sky is vision and direction, the wider weather you share with gods and neighbors. When one realm runs loud and another goes quiet, you know where to put your hands next. Three old witnesses, three rooms of one small life.

Stand or sit where you are and try the three-breath blessing, which takes under a minute. Breathe into your feet for land, your belly for sea, your brow for sky, and say quietly, Power of land, sea, and sky with me. Do it at waking or at the sink before dishes. If held breath ever strains you, speak the words low over tea instead. Small, bodily, repeatable — that is the point.

Lore Root: Land/Sea/Sky oath formula in Irish sources — Attested; three-breath practice — Modern.

Otherworld

Next door, not upstairs

Forget ladders and trapdoors when you picture the Otherworld, because the Irish and Welsh tales set it sideways to this one, like a country next door. The Irish manuscripts name its addresses plainly: Tir na nOg, the land of youth, and Mag Mell, the pleasant plain with its undying feast, and Tech Duinn, the house of Donn where the dead gather beyond the wave. The Welsh tales call it Annwn, the deep place, reached through mist and lake and mound. Near, not above.

Read those addresses as story-truth rather than travel directions, and keep the layers straight. That the tales name these islands and halls is Attested in medieval manuscripts — firm ground. That country folk later spoke of fairy hills and night visitors belongs to folk custom, a likely echo but not the same voice. That you might map it onto your own dreams or streets is Modern, your honest overlay, true to you but not evidence for them. Same door, three keys.

Give it twenty minutes at your table with paper and a pencil. Sketch your own life as a house with one extra door: mark where that door stands, what lies one step beyond it in three small details — a smell, a sound, a light — and who you would trust to knock first. Keep the drawing plain and label it with today's date. You are learning the neighborhood before you visit.

Lore Root: Otherworld islands and halls across Irish and Welsh tales — Attested in medieval manuscripts.

Grimoire prompt

Otherworld

Pwyll trades places

Take the First Branch of the Mabinogi, the great medieval Welsh story collection, and open on Pwyll, a young prince out hunting who offends Arawn, a king of Annwn. To mend it he trades places and shapes with Arawn for a full year, ruling the deep kingdom justly, sleeping chastely beside a strange queen with his back turned each night, and meeting Arawn's enemy in single combat at the ford. He comes home with honor and a lifelong friend. A year of borrowed kingship, cleanly kept.

Hear the tale as a lesson in held boundaries rather than a model for worship. That Pwyll and Arawn trade kingdoms is Attested as medieval Welsh story, not history and not ritual instruction. Nobody converts and nobody conquers here; two kings simply keep each other's houses well for a season, and both peoples prosper for it. Folk and Modern retellings sometimes moralize it further into parable — leave those aside and let the old shape stand on its own feet.

Sit down for half an hour and borrow the prince's audit for your own rooms. Write what kingdom you would be trusted to keep for a year — a flat, a friendship, a workbench — and list three things you would tend, one you would refuse to touch, and the promise you would keep to the letter. Sign it lightly in pencil and revisit it at month's end. Borrowed things teach careful hands.

Lore Root: Pwyll and Arawn in the First Branch of the Mabinogi — Attested medieval Welsh tale.

Grimoire prompt
If you were given a whole year to keep someone else's kingdom, how would you spend it?

How the door opens

The doors keep habits, and once you learn them you start seeing them everywhere in the tales. Burial mounds stand open at Samhain while boats sail west to the islands, mists settle low on hillsides, and a hunter follows a white stag with red ears — the tales' own signal-fires — straight into a court he never looked for. Water and hill and mist and feast-night return again and again across Irish and Welsh stories, always at the seam. Edges, not centers.

Treat that pattern as literary rather than literal, and keep it distinct from later habit. That mounds and mists and white animals mark entries is Attested as a tale-pattern across the manuscripts. That country people still leave certain hills and wells alone belongs to folk custom, suggestive but separate. Your own sense that some streets change at dusk is Modern experience, true to you but no evidence for them. Pattern, echo, life — three different weights.

Give ten minutes to a walk around your own block with soft eyes and a small notebook. Mark three spots where the air seems to change — a stairwell with its own weather, a tree with traffic of its own, a corner gone quieter — and write one line for each about what shifted. No rite and no offering, attention only. When you get home, read the three lines aloud and circle the one that pulls hardest; that is your thin place for the month.

Lore Root: liminal entries across Irish and Welsh tales — Attested as literary pattern.

The rules of visiting

Guests survive the Otherworld on manners, and the tales repeat the house rules so often you can recite them by heart. Eat little or eat knowingly, take nothing without leave, keep your promise to the letter, and never mistake the host's courtesy for your own power. Pwyll keeps every rule and prospers, while boasters and thieves in tale after tale pay exactly the price they were told at the door. Old hospitality, hard edges.

Use those rules as household sense first and story scholarship second. That guest-rules run through the tales is Attested as literary pattern, not a code anyone carved in stone. Folk custom keeps a cousin-shape in gifts left at wells and thresholds to stay on good terms — related, not identical. Modern use here simply turns the mirror around: imagine yourself the host and ask what your own rooms require of strangers. The card on your cupboard is the modern half, and honesty keeps it clean. Tale, custom, table.

Tonight write the three rules you would post on your own door, as if a prince might come to read them. Keep them short enough to fit an index card, plain enough for a tired guest, and kind enough that you would keep them yourself. Tape the card inside a cupboard and live with it a week. If a rule fails by day three, rewrite it shorter instead of scolding yourself; house rules are drafts, and drafts improve.

Lore Root: guest-rules across the tales — Attested as literary pattern.

The veil walk

Start with the doorway you already cross every day, because the old pattern says thresholds listen. Stand in yours and breathe three slow breaths while you name land and sea and sky, then walk ten minutes with soft eyes and empty hands, listening more than looking, and notice everything that feels sideways — a colder step, a bird going quiet, a smell with no source. Walk home the same way and close with a word of thanks. Dawn or dusk sharpens it, when light itself stands between.

Hold this walk for what it is and no more. Doorway and twilight liminality grows from tale-patterns and folk custom, so this book labels the whole exercise Reconstructed practice — an honest modern making, not an old rite handed down. It borrows the tales' love of edges the way you borrow a neighbor's ladder: with thanks, and returned unharmed. What it gives back is steadier notice, not messages. No trance, no summons, nothing bound or commanded.

Choose your time now, dawn or dusk, and your doorway, front or back or stairwell. Set ten minutes by the kitchen timer, leave the phone home, and carry nothing but keys and attention. If weather pins you indoors, walk the hallway instead and note its doors; edges live inside too. When you return, give the walk four lines in this book: where you went, what turned sideways, what stayed ordinary.

Lore Root: doorway and twilight liminality from tale-patterns and folk custom — Reconstructed practice.

Otherworld

Dawn and dusk primer

Half-light carries half the stories, and your own street can show you why. The tales love edges where one thing becomes another: bargains struck at fords between banks, meetings at doorways between rooms, messages arriving in the minute between sleeping and waking, all lit by a low sun or a single candle. Folk custom still honors the seams, keeping dawn water drawn before sunrise and dusk candles lit against the dark in living memory. Neither-times, kept daily.

Separate the layers and both stay useful. That twilight edges fill the tales is Attested as literary motif, and that country households kept dawn and dusk observances belongs to folk custom — suggestive continuities, not proof of one old rite. Sitting five minutes to watch the sky change needs no lineage at all; call it Modern rest, plain and unborrowed, the kind any tired person may keep. Old motif, old habit, present rest.

Pick dawn or dusk and keep it three days running, five minutes each, wherever you can see sky from your flat — window, step, balcony, even a cracked-open door. Keep the same seat each day so change shows. No phone and no rite, just watching while the light does its work and your shoulders drop. Afterward write three lines: what the light did, what sounds came and went, what shifted inside you. Three small sittings make a hinge you can feel swing.

Lore Root: twilight edges in tale and folk custom — Folk survival and literary motif.

Dreaming across

Night hands you the cheapest boat to the other shore, and the tales treat it as traffic rather than trivia. Across the Irish and Welsh manuscripts warnings arrive in dreams, lovers visit sleeping chambers, and the dead speak plainly before dawn, their words steering battles and marriages alike. Whether or not you credit any of that yet, the storytellers took night messages seriously, and wrote them down as counsel. Dreams carried weight.

Keep the old claim and the new habit apart and both stay honest. That dream-visits fill the tales is Attested as literary motif, not proof the old parties kept dream books. Folk dictionaries of dream-meanings are later make, and this book sets them aside entirely. The seven-night log offered here is Modern practice, useful because dates and images show your own weather, not because symbols decode. Your dates, your hand. Motif old, notebook new.

Set this book beside your bed tonight with a pen that writes in the dark, tied to the cover with string so midnight you can find it. When you wake, catch one image before your feet touch the floor — a color, a creature, a sentence — and give it three lines with the date, every night dated even when blank. No dictionaries and no decoding, seven nights running. Patterns matter more than interpretations, and your hand will learn the oar-stroke.

Gods

Keep the cultures separate

Picture three neighboring houses on one lane, related but never the same household, each with its own door and its own manners. In the Gaulish house the gods reach you as short prayers cut in stone, little votive inscriptions set up by soldiers and merchants and townsfolk, with a carved image here and there and almost no story attached. In the Irish and Welsh houses it runs the other way entirely, with rich tales copied by Christian scribes centuries after conversion, full of speech and quarrel and marvel, but thin on altars and offerings. Two kinds of evidence, neither wrong.

Read each house on its own terms and the confusion that fills so many books lifts like mist. More than four hundred local Celtic divine names survive in inscriptions, yet only a handful were ever widely shared, so any page that flattens them into one pantheon is selling you something modern and tidy. Attested stone names belong to Gaul, while the big personalities belong to medieval Irish and Welsh literature, and the bridge between the two is Reconstructed at best. This chapter keeps the doors marked on purpose, Gaulish-named ones first, then Irish, then Welsh, each greeted in their own way and never blended at the table.

Try it tonight at the kitchen table in twenty minutes with a pencil and this book open flat. Walk the chapter in order, door by door, and jot which one pulls you and why in a single honest line. Greet that household first and let the other two wait their turn, the way you would with new neighbors. One open door teaches more than three half-opened ones, and the household you enter properly will lend you its calm and its customs for the rest of the year.

Lore Root: 400+ local names in inscriptions vs. a few pan-regional figures — Attested.

Gods of Gaul

Cernunnos, the horned one

On the Gundestrup cauldron, a great decorated silver vessel found in Denmark, he sits cross-legged and antlered, holding a neck-ring in one hand, ringed round by stag and bull and lion. The antlers rise like bare winter branches above a calm human face, and the beasts press close as if posing for a portrait nobody commissioned. Scholars debate the vessel's makers, but the image itself is plain to anyone who looks. On the Pillar of the Boatmen at Paris his name is carved in stone by river-merchants doing business under Rome, plain letters beside carved gods, a businessman's devotion. No tale tells what he says or wants, and that silence is nearly the whole of him.

Treat that silence as the teaching rather than a gap to fill with invention. He reads as wild plenty that never negotiates and cannot be owned, the deer path and the weed lot going on without you whether you praise it or not. Later stories casting him as a horned god of witches are modern make, worth leaving out here, because the Attested part fits in two objects and the rest is wishful stitching. Two witnesses, one image and one inscription, carry more weight than a shelf of speculation, and this book trusts the two witnesses.

Honor him with attention rather than requests, close to home and often through the year. Stand a full minute where trees and weeds do as they please, a park corner or a cracked pavement bed, name what lives there, carry some litter home in your pocket. Watching can be worship when it is repeated weekly in all weathers, and ten kept minutes at the same spot count for more than an altar you never visit. Go in rain too; the wild does not close for weather, and neither should the watcher.

Lore Root: Gundestrup image plus Pillar of the Boatmen inscription — Attested; all stories about him — Modern.

Cernunnos — practice

Pick one watching spot you can reach on foot, somewhere half-wild is allowed to go on being itself without tidying. A canal bank, a churchyard corner, the unmown strip behind the flats, anywhere with weeds and birds and insects living unconsulted by committees. Go there at the same hour twice in one week and stand still long enough for the place to forget you are there. Learn its regulars by sight before you offer anything at all, the way you would learn neighbors' faces before knocking.

The offering here is manners, not transaction, and that distinction carries the whole rite from start to finish. Pouring a little clean water to the roots, keeping one path clear of rubbish, carrying litter home, naming three wild neighbors aloud, these are Reconstructed gestures built on the old pattern of care at shrines. Nothing is asked for and nothing is bargained, because the wild plenty this god images does not negotiate and would not understand the question. You are a guest showing good manners, nothing grander, and that is enough.

Keep the whole round under half an hour, phone away and hands free and eyes up. Name bird and weed and insect by whatever honest names you have, garden-book or made-up-but-kept, and write them in this book when you get home with the date beside each line. Take every trace of the visit out with you, down to the last crumb and tissue, and let the returning itself become the vow you keep.

Epona of the horses

Soldiers carried her name across the map, from Britain to Bulgaria, cut in stone wherever cavalrymen served far from home. Epona is a Gaulish horse-goddess, and her dedications rank among the best-attested Celtic cults anywhere in bronze and stone, set up by riders who depended on their animals daily for life itself. Picture a tired trooper in a strange province paying a carver for her image, wanting his mare watched over through the winter campaign. She guards horses and riders, travelers and stables alike, steady and unsentimental, asking clean straw rather than poetry.

Almost no stories of her survive, so her worship reads best as stable manners rather than mythology, and that is a relief rather than a lack. Care of working animals, care of the road, care of fellow travelers, that is the household shape of her, and it fits any century with hooves or wheels in it. Attested dedications give you the whom and where; the how is Reconstructed from the life around horses, and it always comes down to hands doing the work instead of mouths making promises. Grooming beats hymns every time.

Translate that into your own week without needing a horse of your own or a stable. Groom something that works hard, your bicycle or your boots or an old dog, walk one errand you would normally ride, feed the working animals first before you sit down to eat. She reads hands better than altars, and a brushed coat or a mended strap says the prayer for you more plainly than any verse you could memorize.

Lore Root: dedications across the Roman world — Attested; stories — none survive.

Epona — practice

Start with the road you actually travel, not an imagined Roman highway with cypresses. Her old gifts were simple things left where horses and travelers pass, milk or water, bread or apples, and the gift still matters less than the work beside it. Check a bridle, pick a hoof, oil a chain, pump a tire, carry a traveler's bag one stop further than comfort suggests. Let your hands learn the shape of the work before your mouth describes it.

Make the kindness specific and road-shaped, because vague goodwill evaporates by Tuesday and helps nobody at all. Drive gently past riders, yield to the horse-box on narrow lanes, refill the water bowl at the stable gate, sweep the shared entry where wheels jam every morning. Each small road-kindness belongs to her pattern, Reconstructed but honest, work-as-offering rather than words-as-offering, and the street notices even when no one thanks you. Especially then.

Close the loop in this book within the week, under an hour all told including the errand itself. Write what you gave, where you left it, and what your hands did besides the giving, with a date beside each line for the record. The record matters more than the poetry here, since her old worship survives in ledgers of care rather than in hymns, and ledgers want dates and details. Ride or walk the same road again next week, and add a second line beneath the first.

Taranis of the wheel

Lucan, a Roman poet writing about Gaul, names Taranis among the gods of the Gauls, and stones across the old provinces carve him beside his wheel. The wheel reads as sun and thunder and turning year all at once, spokes holding the sky's motion in one round sign you can trace with a finger on the page. Wherever Romans met him they paired him with Jupiter, their own sky-father, which tells you how seriously the sky was taken by everyone laboring under it. Thunder was never decoration; it was the roof of the world talking.

Thunder gods ask for upright conduct more than for poetry, and that is the household meaning to carry home from this page. Keep oaths, pay debts, offer shelter when storms come, stand straight while the sky is loud and do not use the noise as cover for crookedness. The Attested part is the name and the wheel and the Roman pairing; the character notes are Reconstructed, drawn from what thunder has always demanded of people huddled beneath it. Straight dealing is the oldest lightning rod.

Picture the next storm as his audit rather than his anger, a yearly review arriving early and unannounced. Step under solid cover, listen it through to the end without fidgeting, review one promise you made and whether it still holds water. No drama is needed, only exactness, because thunder hears everything and forgets nothing, and the review ends when the rain does.

Lore Root: Lucan plus wheel-inscriptions — Attested; character notes — Reconstructed.

Teutates, protector of the people

Toutatis in some inscriptions, Teutates in Lucan, the name itself means the god of the tribe, the people as one body rather than one hero on a horse. Lucan, the Roman poet who names the gods of Gaul, names him among them, and the dedications read like civic prayer for folk and town and borderland. Call on him when the trouble is shared and nobody's job in particular: the damp stairwell everyone ignores, the rota nobody covers, the street that floods every autumn while the council looks away.

His worship is neighbor-work, plain and regular, and it begins at your own front door rather than at some distant shrine. Learn your neighbors' names and use them, keep the shared path swept, show up for the unglamorous meeting where chairs outnumber speeches and the biscuits go stale by nine. Protection here is collective or it is nothing at all, and the Attested name-meaning backs that reading without needing any modern gloss or borrowed ceremony. The tribe is whoever shares your postcode.

Try him as a habit rather than a hymn, one small civic act each week without fail. One remembered name used aloud, one swept landing, one attended meeting a month, small change that compounds quietly like interest. Tribes run on records of care kept over years, and your street is tribe enough to start with tonight, exactly as it is and exactly as you are.

Bread and drink open this one, set out for the household with an extra portion for whoever passes by, walker or driver or delivery rider in the rain.
