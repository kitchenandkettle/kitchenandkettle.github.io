Working with the Old Ways

Rest your tired bones and troubled mind. This book shares the old Norse ways, which were about keeping good company with the living, the dead, and the land. It is meant to bring you comfort and a sense of connection with your ancestors. If parts of it do not fit your life or beliefs, leave them behind and keep what helps. I have tried to present these old ways as accurately as the sources allow, and I mark each claim with where it comes from. Use the lines on each page to write, reflect, and build your own practice in whatever way works best for you.

This book mentions plants from folk custom and from medieval medical books, but it does not replace a doctor. Never eat, drink, or apply any plant from these pages based on this book alone. If you forage, go with an experienced guide, check each plant against at least three trusted sources, and take only what you need, leaving most of it behind. For medicinal herbs, I buy from Mountain Rose Herbs: https://mountainroseherbs.com/

If a source is late, or scholars disagree, the note says so. Anything described as modern or rebuilt was written for your home and is not an old practice. The main sources are the Poetic Edda, Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda, the medieval rune poems, and the sagas listed in Resources.

How to say the names

Old Norse names look hard, but a few rules cover most of them. The letter Þ (þ) sounds like “th” in thin. The letter Ð (ð) sounds like “th” in this. An accent mark over a vowel means hold it a little longer. The letter ö sounds like the “u” in fur, and j sounds like English “y.” The final -r on names like Þórr, Baldr, and Freyr is just a grammar ending, and English often drops it.

The Poems

The Poems Behind This Book

This book leans on a handful of old texts, and you will meet their names on almost every page. Here is what they are. The Poetic Edda is a collection of about thirty poems about gods and heroes. Most of them survive in one Icelandic manuscript, the Codex Regius, copied around 1270. Their poets are unknown. The poems were probably composed between about 900 and 1200 and kept by memory first, so their dates are best guesses.

The Prose Edda was written by Snorri Sturluson, an Icelandic chieftain and poet, around 1220. He wrote it as a handbook for young poets, retelling the myths in plain prose so they could follow the old verse. Snorri was a Christian writing two centuries after Iceland converted, so his version is useful, tidy, and late. Where he and the poems disagree, this book says so.

Two more kinds of text appear here. Icelandic sagas are prose stories about families and kings, written down in the 1200s and later. The medieval rune poems are short verses, one for each rune. The next pages give a short guide to the poems you will meet most often. You do not need to read them first. Come back to this guide whenever a title puzzles you.

Where this comes from: The Codex Regius dates to about 1270 and Snorri’s Prose Edda to about 1220. The dates of the individual poems are debated.

The Poems

The Lokasenna: Loki’s quarrel

The Lokasenna is a flyting, a contest of insults, and it is the poem this book cites most when it speaks of Loki. The sea-giant Ægir (Aegir) brews ale for the gods and holds a feast. Þórr is away in the east. Loki has already killed one of Ægir’s servants and been chased out, but he walks back in and demands a seat.

One by one he insults the gods, accusing them of cowardice and unfaithfulness. They answer him, and he has a cruel reply for each. At the end Þórr arrives and threatens him with his hammer, and Loki leaves. Two stanzas show the poem’s edge. First Loki reminds Óðinn of their bond: “Odin! dost thou remember when we in early days blended our blood together?” Then Þórr, on arriving: “Silence, thou impure being! My mighty hammer, Miollnir, shall stop thy prating. I will thy head from thy neck strike; then will thy life be ended.” (Stanzas 9 and 57, translated by Benjamin Thorpe, 1866.)

A short prose ending in the manuscript tells what follows. The gods catch Loki, who had hidden as a salmon, and bind him with the entrails of his son. Skaði hangs a serpent over his face. His wife, Sigyn, holds a bowl to catch the venom. When she turns away to empty it, the venom drips on him, and his struggling shakes the earth.

Where this comes from: The Lokasenna and its prose ending are in the Poetic Edda. The excerpts are Benjamin Thorpe’s 1866 translation, now public domain.

The Poems

The Völuspá: the seeress speaks

The Völuspá opens with a seeress asking for silence. “For silence I pray all sacred children, great and small, sons of Heimdall, they will that I Valfather’s deeds recount, men’s ancient saws, those that I best remember.” (Stanza 1, translated by Benjamin Thorpe, 1866.) Valfather is Óðinn. She remembers the giants, nine worlds, and the great tree, and then the time before the world: “There was in times of old, where Ymir dwelt, nor sand nor sea, nor gelid waves; earth existed not, nor heaven above, ’twas a chaotic chasm, and grass nowhere.” (Stanza 3.)

The poem moves through the making of the world, the first people, the war of the gods, the death of Baldr, and the coming of Ragnarök. After the doom, it turns: “She sees arise, a second time, earth from ocean, beauteously green.” (Stanza 57.) “There shall again the wondrous golden tables in the grass be found.” (Stanza 59.) The seeress keeps asking, “Understand ye yet, or what?”

The last stanzas, about a mighty judge from above, appear only in a later manuscript and are usually thought to show Christian influence. Read the poem aloud once, slowly, and notice where it begins and where it ends.

Where this comes from: The Völuspá in the Poetic Edda, in Benjamin Thorpe’s 1866 translation, now public domain.

Who Were the Old Norse

The Old Norse were the people of Scandinavia and the North Atlantic, mostly from about 750 to 1050 CE, a time called the Viking Age. They were not one kingdom. They lived on farms, islands, and fjords in what is now Norway, Denmark, Sweden, and Iceland, and the sea was their main road. They spoke Old Norse, a North Germanic language, and cut it in runes, letters carved into stone and wood.

We know them through two kinds of record. Stones and graves show what people left behind: names, burials, tools, and ships. Books tell the stories, and they were written down later, mostly in Iceland. The Poetic Edda is a collection of old poems about gods and heroes. Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda retells the myths in plain prose. Icelandic sagas are prose stories about families and kings.

Stones keep what people finished, and stories keep what people cared about. You need both. On each page I will tell you which kind of record I am using.

Where this comes from: Runestones and the Viking Age dates come from archaeology. The Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda were copied down centuries later.

Who Were the Old Norse

No Viking wrote a handbook of belief for us to find. Most of what we know comes from people who were not Vikings, or who lived long after. The Roman writer Tacitus described Germanic peoples around 98 CE, from outside, for a Roman audience. The Arab traveler Ibn Fadlan watched a chieftain’s funeral on the Volga River in 922 and wrote it down as a stranger. Icelandic Christians copied the old poems and sagas onto vellum, a prepared animal skin, in the 1200s. They saved the voices, but they changed the frame.

That means the record has gaps, and this book says so. It is about the Old Norse only. It is not Celtic lore with Norse names, and it is not a modern mix pretending to be old. When modern people have filled a gap, I say so plainly, and I name the source of everything else. Every lore page ends with a note called “Where this comes from.”

Ask who wrote each source and why, and you will know how far to trust it.

Where this comes from: How this book works. Its method is modern, but it is built on old sources.

The wary guest

The Hávamál (Havamal), the “Sayings of the High One,” is a poem in the Poetic Edda full of advice from Óðinn. It values a clear head over a high mood. Stanza 7 describes the wise guest: “A wary guest, who to refection comes, keeps a cautious silence, with his ears listens, and with his eyes observes: so explores every prudent man.” (Translated by Benjamin Thorpe, 1866.)

The practice is the wary guest’s. Before you enter a room, a meeting, or a hard conversation this week, stop at the door. Stay quiet at first, listen with your ears, and watch with your eyes. Notice what is happening before you decide what to say.

Do this once a day for a week, and write one line each time on what you noticed. A clear head matters too: the Hávamál warns that too much drink steals the mind. Test the old advice for yourself.

Where this comes from: The counsel and the quotation are from the Hávamál in the Poetic Edda, in Benjamin Thorpe’s 1866 translation, now public domain. The practice builds on stanza 7.

Nine Realms

Creation: fire meets mist

Before anything had a name, there was a gap. The Old Norse called it Ginnungagap, probably “the yawning gap.” It was not a person but a place: a huge empty space with fire on one side and ice on the other. Múspell (Muspell), the fire world, burned at one edge, guarded by the giant Surtr (Surt) with a flaming sword. Niflheimr (Niflheim), the mist world, lay at the other, where poisonous rivers froze into frost.

Then fire and ice met. Sparks from Múspell crossed the gap and melted the frost into drops, and the drops became Ymir, the first giant. A cow named Auðumla (Audumla) fed on the salty ice until a man named Búri (Buri) appeared. He was the grandfather of Óðinn (Odin). Óðinn and his brothers killed Ymir and made the world from his body: earth from his flesh, sea from his blood, sky from his skull, mountains from his bones, and trees from his hair.

People came last. In Snorri Sturluson’s Gylfaginning, three gods found two fallen trees on a shore, Ask and Embla, ash and elm. The gods gave them breath, wit, and warmth, and the trees stood up as the first people.

Where this comes from: The creation story is from the Völuspá (Voluspa), Vafþrúðnismál (Vafthrudnismal), and Grímnismál (Grimnismal) in the Poetic Edda, and from Snorri’s Prose Edda.

Nine Realms

Nine Realms: sky, middle, and wild

The Old Norse told of nine realms, or worlds, held together by one great tree, Yggdrasill (Yggdrasil). They were not stacked like floors. Ásgarðr (Asgard) is the high home of the Æsir (Aesir) gods, such as Óðinn and Þórr, reached by the rainbow bridge Bifröst (Bifrost). Miðgarðr (Midgard) is the middle world where people live. Jörmungandr (Jormungandr), the world serpent, coils in the sea around its edge.

Vanaheimr (Vanaheim) is the home of the Vanir, an older family of gods tied to harvest and plenty, such as Njörðr (Njord), Freyr, and Freyja. Jötunheimr (Jotunheim) is the wild land of the jötnar (giants), who stand for chaos and untamed nature. The Æsir and the giants are often at odds.

Neither the Poetic Edda nor Snorri’s Prose Edda gives one map of the nine realms. The ring-shaped diagram you may have seen is a modern way to draw them. The next page names the rest: Álfheimr, Svartálfaheimr, Múspell, Niflheimr, and Hel.

Where this comes from: The realms and Bifröst are named in the Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda. The ring-shaped map was drawn for this book.

Nine Realms: light, dark, fire, ice, and the dead

Álfheimr (Alfheim) is the bright home of the light elves. Svartálfaheimr (Svartalfheim), also called Niðavellir (Nidavellir) in some sources, is the underground home of the dwarfs and dark elves, whose smiths forged the gods’ finest treasures.

Múspell is the world of fire, ruled by the giant Surtr. Niflheimr (Niflheim) is the world of ice and fog. At its center is the spring Hvergelmir, and one root of Yggdrasill reaches down to it. The two worlds stand on opposite sides of the gap where the world was made.

Hel is the world of the dead, ruled by a giantess also named Hel. In Snorri’s Prose Edda she receives those who die of old age or sickness, not in battle. This book does not describe the road there. The practice is simpler: keep the names of your dead in use, as you do on the Ancestors pages.

Where this comes from: These realms are named in the Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda. The name Niðavellir and the sorting of elves into light and dark come from later sources.

Nine Realms

Yggdrasill: the ash that holds

Yggdrasill is a giant ash tree that holds the nine worlds together. Its three roots reach three wells: Hvergelmir in Niflheimr, the well of Mímir (Mimir), and the well of Urðr (Urd), where the Nornir (Norns) sit and shape fate. A stag feeds on its shoots above. The squirrel Ratatoskr (Ratatosk) runs up and down the trunk carrying messages, and he scolds both ends as he goes.

Read the tree as a picture of how things connect, not as a road map. No source describes a home ritual for it, so this book does not invent one. What you can take home is simple. Everything depends on something else. Roots need water. News travels the trunk like gossip. What you feed at the roots shows up later in the leaves.

Give your roots steady care, such as sleep, regular meals, and honest talk. Notice which roots are dry this week, and water one of them on purpose.

Where this comes from: The tree and its tenants are described in the Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda.

Nine Realms

Where the dead go: Valhöll (Valhalla), Fólkvangr (Folkvangr), Hel

The dead do not all go to one place. Valhöll (Valhalla) is Óðinn’s hall, roofed with shields and raftered with spears, and it takes half of those slain in battle. Freyja takes the other half to Fólkvangr (Folkvangr), her own field. Hel keeps those who die of sickness or old age in her fenced hall, which is neither burning nor shining. A grim shore is set aside for oath-breakers and murderers. Baldr went down to Hel, and he stayed there after the ransom failed.

Valhöll and Fólkvangr appear in the Poetic Edda. Hel’s hall appears in Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda and in the Poetic Edda, both written down late. Burial mounds tell a plainer story. They hold grave goods, ship settings, and feasting gear, which show care for the journey. Stories and mound earth are two kinds of witness. Neither cancels the other, and you can hold both while you grieve.

Your memory of someone is a form of care. It can last longer than any set of beliefs. You do not need the right belief to honor them. You need to keep their name in use. Speak it, write it, and keep one thing they loved in your home.

Where this comes from: Valhöll, Fólkvangr, and Hel are described in the Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda, both copied down late. The mounds are known from archaeology.

Nine Realms

Ragnarok: winter without spring

Ragnarök (Ragnarok) is the end of the world, told in the Völuspá. First comes Fimbulwinter, three winters in a row with no summer between. Families break their promises to one another, and an age of axes begins. The sun darkens and the stars fall. Naglfar, a ship made of dead men’s nails, floats free. Heimdallr (Heimdall) blows his horn, and the gods ride out to the plain of Vígríðr (Vigrid).

There they fight. Óðinn falls to the wolf Fenrir. Þórr (Thor) kills the world serpent and falls dead nine steps later. Loki and Heimdallr kill each other. Fire burns the world.

But the poem does not stop there. Earth rises green from the sea a second time. Baldr and Höðr (Hodr) return, and Víðarr (Vidar) and Váli (Vali) live on. The golden game pieces the gods once played with are found again in the grass. When something in your life ends, look for what grows after it. Name the ending, then find one small sign of new growth. You can grieve the loss and still look for it.

Where this comes from: The doom and the greening are from the Völuspá, retold in Snorri’s Prose Edda. This book offers no rites for the end of the world.

Nine Realms

Doors: mounds and edges

In many old stories you cross into another world at an edge, not at a center. In Icelandic stories, burial mounds are said to stand open at Yule, the midwinter feast, and the dead walk near the living. A mound, a shoreline, and a feast night are all places where one world touches another.

Treat this as a story pattern, not a literal gate. Mounds mark crossing points in Icelandic sagas, and the Yule opening comes mostly from later Icelandic folk belief. Some farms still leave certain hills and landing places alone. That is folk custom, related to the stories but separate from them. Your own street can feel different at dusk. That feeling is real, but it is your own, not proof about other worlds.

Use the pattern to pay attention. Go out at dusk and notice the edges near you: a gate, a water line, a crossing. Write down what changes at each one.

Where this comes from: Mounds as doors appear in Icelandic sagas. The idea that they open at Yule comes from later Icelandic folk belief.

Crooked time: promises across winter

The Old Norse did not always tell a story in a straight line. The Völuspá starts at the beginning of the world, jumps forward into doom, then past it into green earth rising again. Their year worked the same way. Icelanders counted years in winters, and oaths sworn at Yule were meant to hold through the summer, so a vow spoken on a winter night came due in spring.

Your own year works this way too. School terms and work rotas set the pace, and anniversaries arrive before you feel ready. A promise made in winter comes due in spring.

Pick one promise you made this winter and check it against the spring. Ask whether it still stands, and what it asks of you now. A written promise is easier to keep than a remembered one. Put a reminder on your calendar for the first warm day of spring.

Where this comes from: The looping style is in the Völuspá and Icelandic sagas, and winter-counting is in the sagas.

Rǫgn: Gods

Æsir and Vanir: The God Tribes

Rǫgn is an old Norse word for the powers: the gods and the forces that rule the world. The gods come in two families. The Æsir include Óðinn, Þórr, and Frigg. They live in Ásgarðr, the high realm in the sky, and they care about oath, battle, and order. The Vanir include Njörðr, Freyr, and Freyja. They live in Vanaheimr and care about the sea, the harvest, and plenty. Giants and elves live in other realms and belong to neither family.

The two families once fought a war and then made peace. The next page tells how it ended. Scholars think the story may remember two peoples who later joined together. It comes to us from the Völuspá, the seeress’s prophecy in the Poetic Edda, and from Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda, written around 1220. Both were written down centuries after the Viking Age, so the details are uncertain.

Think of the two families as two households that learned to share one valley. Each keeps its own job. The Æsir keep oath and order, and the Vanir keep work and plenty. A home that has only one of them is missing something.

Where this comes from: The Æsir and Vanir are in the Völuspá (Poetic Edda) and Snorri’s Prose Edda, both copied late. The idea that they were once two peoples is a scholars’ theory, not proven.

Grimoire prompt

The Æsir–Vanir war

The Æsir and Vanir once fought a war. The Völuspá says the Æsir broke a truce, and the Vanir fought back. Neither side could win for good, so they made peace.

The peace was built on hostages, a person from each side sent to live with the other. Njörðr and Freyr went to live among the Æsir. Hænir (Hoenir) and Mímir (Mimir) went to the Vanir. A hostage was a guest, fed and treated well, and that gave both sides a reason to keep their word. A promise is easy to break. A person sitting at the other table is harder to forget.

Njörðr and Freyr spent time in Ásgarðr as part of the treaty, but their home is Vanaheimr. The stories do not describe life after the treaty, so think of it this way: each family has its own home and its own job, and they meet at the table. The Völuspá and Snorri’s Prose Edda tell this as medieval Icelandic story, not as history of a real war.

Where this comes from: The war and the hostage exchange are told in the Völuspá and Snorri’s Prose Edda, both copied late.

Óðinn: the one-eyed

Óðinn (Odin) is the chief of the Æsir, the high one, and the god of wisdom, poetry, war, and the dead. He rides the eight-legged horse Sleipnir, shown on picture stones and thin gold medallions called bracteates. Two ravens, Huginn and Muninn, fly out each day and bring him news: thought and memory. Two wolves, Geri and Freki, follow at his heels. The Eddas also call him Alföðr (Allfather).

He paid a high price for wisdom. He gave one eye to the well of Mímir (Mimir) for a drink of its water. He hung nine nights on Yggdrasill, wounded by his own spear, to win the runes. The Hávamál gives his long counsel on wits and measure. The Poetic Edda shows him generous and terrible by turns, and he breaks oaths too.

Later books make him a kindly Allfather for modern worship, which is a modern softening. Jackson Crawford, a modern scholar of Old Norse, has argued that the name Alföðr may mean ‘All Order’ rather than ‘All-Father’. This reading is debated, and this book does not settle it.

The lesson is in the price. Óðinn wants to know things, and that hunger never comes free. Steady attention counts for more than an altar nobody visits. Give a set hour each week to learning a real craft, a poem, or a history.

Frigg: wife and seer

Frigg is the wife of Óðinn and the mother of Baldr. She sits beside the high seat and sees further than she tells. She knows what will come and speaks of it to almost no one. She spins and weaves and keeps the keys and inner doors of the household.

Loki: The Trickster

No devil stands here, whatever later sermons made of him. Snorri’s Gylfaginning says Loki was born a giant, the son of Fárbauti and Laufey, and the old texts sometimes name him for his mother, Laufey’s son. He lives among the Æsir in Ásgarðr, and in the Lokasenna he reminds Óðinn that the two swore blood-brotherhood. With the giantess Angrboða he fathered Hel, the wolf Fenrir, and the world serpent. His mischief starts small and turns costly. In Snorri’s Prose Edda he cuts off the golden hair of Sif, the wife of Þórr, and Þórr threatens to break every bone in him. Loki goes to the dwarfs for new hair, and the gifts he brings back include Þórr’s hammer. The harm grows. He arranges the death of Baldr, and in the Lokasenna he insults every god at a feast until Þórr threatens him with his hammer and he leaves. At last the gods bind him in a cave with a serpent’s venom dripping on him. At Ragnarök he breaks free. He is clever, cruel, and useful.

He is cleverness without loyalty, and he cannot be flattered into favors. If trouble with him comes near, bring repair and nothing else. Own the mess and repair it fully, facing the person you hurt directly. The binding and the insults are told in the Poetic Edda and Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda. Soft modern readings that make him a misunderstood friend belong elsewhere, not here.

Clear thinking does cold work here, like a knife paring rot from wood. List your tangles. Strike the ones that are only noise. Keep the one that is truly a mess to mend. Clarity is the only answer he respects. One cleanly repaired tangle is worth more than a year of excuses.

Days of the week

The Romans invented our seven-day week and named each day for a god or heavenly body: the Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, and Saturn. In the first centuries CE, Germanic peoples, the ancestors of the Norse and English, began using it.

They kept the week but not the Roman gods. For each Roman god they chose the Germanic god who seemed closest.

Sunday · sunnudagr
Sól, the sun goddess, who drives the sun across the sky.

Monday · mánadagr
Máni, the moon god and Sól’s brother, who drives the moon across the sky.

Tuesday · týsdagr
Týr, god of oaths and law. He was also a god of battle and victory, so he was matched with Rome’s Mars, god of war.

Wednesday · óðinsdagr
Óðinn, the high one. He wandered in disguise, ruled poetry and speech, and received the dead, so he was matched with Rome’s Mercury, god of speech.

Thursday · þórsdagr
Þórr, the thunderer. He ruled thunder and carried the hammer Mjölnir, so he was matched with Rome’s Jupiter, who wielded the thunderbolt.

Friday · frjádagr
Frigg, Óðinn’s wife and goddess of marriage, so she was matched with Rome’s Venus, goddess of love. Some scholars think Freyja.

Saturday · laugardagr
The Norse named it for bathing, not a god. Laug means “bath,” so laugardagr is “washing day.” English kept Rome’s Saturn.

Where this comes from: The Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda.

Landvættir (landvaettir) are land spirits, the unseen guardians of a place. Icelandic stories and law treat them as real neighbors who must be respected. Blot: simply

A blót (blot) was an old offering feast. In Hákonar saga góða, part of Snorri Sturluson’s Heimskringla, people gather and a gift is given. Blood is caught and sprinkled with twigs on the walls and on the people. Toasts are drunk in order: to the gods, to the ancestors, and to victory. Then everyone feasts on the gift itself. The full cup is the toast to the gods, and the minni cup is the toast to memory, passed hand to hand.

The old hall shrinks to a table here, and this book does not pretend to rebuild it. The modern blot has three acts. Set out a gift of water, milk, bread, song, or work. So give no more than you can spare, and ask for nothing back.

The whole practice fits inside half an hour. Nothing is buried, nothing is poured where it fouls water, and no coins are left in springs. Carry out every trace before you sleep. Repeat it at the next turn of the month. Passing the cup from hand to hand keeps memory alive.

Iðunn’s apples

Iðunn (Idunn) keeps the apples that keep the gods young. One day Óðinn, Loki, and Hœnir are traveling and cook an ox that will not cook. An eagle in a tree says it will cook if it gets its share. Þjazi chases them in eagle form. The gods light a fire on the wall of Ásgarðr, the eagle’s feathers burn, and he falls and is killed. That killing brings his daughter Skaði to Ásgarðr, as her page tells.

Vitki: Rune-Reader

A vitki is a person who works with runes. The old word means something close to “wise one.” The clearest picture comes from Óðinn himself in the Hávamál. He hangs nine nights in a wind-tossed tree, hungry and thirsty, until he sees the runes below and seizes them with a shout. Then he carves them into wood and reddens the cuts with color.

In the Sigrdrífumál, the valkyrie Sigrdrífa, whom Völsunga saga calls Brynhildr, teaches the hero Sigurðr the same craft. She puts victory runes on the sword hilt, ale runes on the drinking horn, and birth runes on the palm. Knowing here is handwork: you cut, you color, and you speak it aloud.

The medieval rune poems (Norwegian, Icelandic, and Anglo-Saxon) are the alphabet book behind the craft. They hold one short verse for each rune letter, each tied to a plain thing: wealth must move, ice bears no weight, the sun steadies. What they never show is anyone laying runes out to tell fortunes. That is modern and should be called so.

Learn one rune letter a week from the rune poems. Cut or draw it on a scrap of wood, and speak its verse aloud. Runes were cut on tools, weapons, and cups to name their owner or maker, so set what you learn to one honest use: mark a tool handle with your own name in runes.

In old stories, the oak is a central, steady tree. Put your palm on the bark. It feels ridged, cold, and steady. A lone oak in a field often stands for generations. Stand close and feel its size.

The oak takes its time. Season after season it adds slow, strong wood. An old oak can keep standing with a hollow trunk. This tree teaches you to think in seasons, not days.

Watching an oak through the seasons keeps you steady. Guess its girth (width around the trunk), and note the birds, year after year. One oak followed through all four seasons gives your year a steady point to return to.

Where this comes from: Viking ships such as Oseberg and Gokstad were built mostly of oak. The oak stanza is in the Anglo-Saxon rune poem.

Wells and the gifts left in them

Bog water and spring water have kept things for a very long time. Long ago, people laid swords, cauldrons, and brooches in lakes, bogs, and springs. Diggers still find them where rivers slow and springs rise. Scholars call these gifts votive deposits, objects given on purpose to a place. In the Völuspá, even Óðinn gives to water: he pledged an eye to the well of Mímir for a drink of wisdom. What mattered most was the giving itself.

A well is a place with its own history. It is not a machine that grants wishes. Old deposits are well documented: they were found, lifted from the ground, and recorded. Tossing coins into a living spring is a newer habit, and coins can pollute the water. Never drink wild water, because you cannot know what is in it.

When you visit, go as a guest of the place. Ask leave before you lift any water, and sit a while to listen before you leave. Take nothing away from the site, and leave nothing behind that will not rot or wash clean. Keep any promise you made to the place. The visit takes under an hour.

Where this comes from: Old gifts in lakes and springs are found by archaeology. Óðinn’s pledge at the well of Mímir is in the Völuspá.

Liminal Places

Sea roads

For the Old Norse, the sea was a road. In Icelandic sagas, it links farms that could never meet by land. Voyages often begin with vows and end with thanks, and some sagas mention gifts to the sea and wind. A shore belongs to neither farm, and open water is where hard bargains and farewells are made. Promises made on a crossing are held to account: people check whether they were kept.

No shore owns the middle of the water, so every promise made there must be said out loud and kept. Crews name what they leave and what they carry, and they speak their terms while still on the water. To reach the far bank, say your terms plainly and keep them.

Many people now live with an unfinished move, a half-paid house, or a task that was never finished. The old idea of a crossing still helps. Every crossing has a bank behind you and a bank ahead. Say your terms aloud to one witness, as the crews did: one thing you carry over and one thing you set down. The crossing is finished when you keep the terms you set.

Where this comes from: Sea-voyage vows appear in Icelandic sagas as a story pattern.

The door and the guest

The Hávamál begins at a doorway. “All door-ways, before going forward, should be looked to; for difficult it is to know where foes may sit within a dwelling.” (Stanza 1, translated by Benjamin Thorpe, 1866.) The poem is about what a traveler does at a door, and what a host owes the traveler who arrives.

A host owes a guest what the guest needs. “Fire is needful to him who is come in, and whose knees are frozen; food and raiment a man requires.” “Water to him is needful who for refection comes, a towel and hospitable invitation.” (Stanzas 3 and 4.) And: “With insult or derision treat thou never a guest or wayfarer.” (Stanza 134.)

Keep your own door this way. Look to it before you go out and when you come in. Have water, a towel, warmth, and food ready for whoever arrives, and welcome them plainly. Once a month, look over your doorway: the lock, the light, and the step.

Dawn, dusk, and Yule night

Twice a day, the light is neither fully day nor fully night. Once a year, at Yule (midwinter), the season does something similar. In Icelandic folk belief, the mounds stood open on Yule night and the dead could walk near the living. Dawn and dusk carry a smaller daily version of that opening. The house is quiet, the edges show clearly, and birds wake or settle as the light shifts. Even a city window shows the change.

In old stories, edges were meeting times. People meet at the shore, where land meets water. Bargains were made and messages passed, and dreams carried warnings that guided voyages and killings. Dream warnings are a story device in the sagas, not proof that anyone kept dream books, and this book does not decode dreams. The phrase “thin places” for such spots is modern. Showing up on time at the edge was the offering.

The old practice for keeping an edge is útiseta, sitting out alone at dusk or dawn, described earlier in this book. Take it in the same small and safe way: three dawns or three dusks in a row, ten minutes each, at a window or on a step. Notice the light and count three sounds. If cloud hides the sun, keep the same hour anyway.

Some old northern and Old English texts blame certain sicknesses on outside beings, such as elves (álfar) and the mara, a night-presser who sits on sleepers. This book takes only the habit of watching the edges. It does not diagnose or treat illness. If you are unwell, see a clinician.

Where this comes from: Meetings at edges and dream warnings appear in Icelandic sagas as story patterns. The phrase “thin places” is modern.

Runes

Runes cut on stone

Run your finger along a stone’s cuts in low light and you feel the shape before you read it. A rune is a letter of the old runic alphabet. The Elder row, the older set in order, has twenty-four runes. They are cut on spearheads, on gold bracteates (thin gold medallions), and on standing stones, from roughly the second to the eighth century. The row begins fehu-uruz-þurisaz-ansuz-raidō-kenaz. Those six runes give the row its name, futhark, which is taken from its first six letters. The Kylver stone on Gotland carries the whole Elder row in order. Centuries later, the Younger row uses sixteen signs, in short-twig and long-branch forms. It was cut from roughly the ninth to the twelfth century on stones such as Rök and Jelling.

The two rows differ in length, not in purpose. The Elder row spells things out fully. Its twenty-four runes are grouped into three families of eight. Later books call them the kin of Freyr, Hagall, and Týr. The Younger row shortens the writing to sixteen signs and leaves more to the reader. Stones give family names and memories. They say a stone was raised for a father. They never give spells or fortunes. The medieval rune poems (Norwegian, Icelandic, and Anglo-Saxon) pair each rune with a short saying. Wealth must move. Ice gives no grip. A torch shows the way. Read each saying slowly, one at a time.

Your hands can learn the runes before your mind does. The first cuts were made by hands holding chisels, and hands still learn them best. Draw straight lines with a ruler, say each name aloud, and count the branches on each rune. Using your hand and your voice together helps the shapes stick. If the whole row feels like too much, start with the first six runes.

Where this comes from: The rows are cut on stones from the 2nd to the 12th century, and the rune poems give the verses. The study practice is modern.

Seven runes for your journal

This book uses seven short runes. Each rune has a saying that the medieval rune poems give. Fehu is cattle and wealth, which must move to stay alive. Uruz is the strong aurochs, a wild ox, pulling through mud. Þurisaz (Thurisaz) is the thorn that guards by pricking whoever grips it. Ansuz is the mouth of Óðinn, speaking counsel. Raidō (Raidho) is the road and riding, which can be smooth or hard on the feet. Kenaz is the torch that shows the evening work. Hagalaz is the hail that breaks roofs and bows to no one. Say each name aloud.

These rune names and sayings come from the medieval rune poems. The Norwegian, Icelandic, and Anglo-Saxon rune poems each speak in their own voice. The set is narrowed to seven because it suits a journal rather than a temple. The book does not claim that Viking Age people used them any other way than as letters with names.

Learn them the way the rune poems were learned, by saying them. Copy each rune with its name and saying onto one line of your journal, then say all seven aloud from memory once a day for a week. When a saying fits your day, note it.

Staves

Galdrastaves are late; use them honestly

Galdrastaves are late. They are not Viking Age runes. A galdrastave is a magic sign drawn in a charm book (a book of spells and charms). The earliest bound staves in Iceland appear in AM 434 a 12mo, an Icelandic charm book of the late 1400s, drawn beside remedies. The books many people know are early modern. Galdrabók (Galdrabok) dates to the 17th century. Lbs 4375 8vo dates to the 1860s. They mix a Christian frame, with Latin, runes, and pictures together.

Ægishjálmr (Aegishjalmr), Helm of Awe, has old poetry behind it: the Fáfnismál names a helm of terror. Vegvísir (Vegvisir), the way-pointer, dates to the 1860s and appears in the Huld manuscript, a 19th-century Icelandic collection. It is not a Viking Age sign. In books such as Galdrabók, the staves draw their power by calling on Christ, Mary, and angels alongside older names. Calling them Viking amulets is a modern label. Kept in their own period, they stay accurate.

Study them as the manuscripts show them. Copy one stave from a reproduction of AM 434 a 12mo or Galdrabók onto paper, and write the date, the book, and the purpose the book gives beside it.

Where this comes from: The staves come from AM 434 a, Galdrabók (17th century), and the Huld manuscript (1860s), all late.

Charms

Galdr: the sung charm

A charm is a short set of words said or sung for a purpose. A galdr is a charm that is sung or spoken aloud, not one thought in silence. In the Hávamál, Óðinn lists the songs he knows, the way a carpenter lists tools, and each one has its work named: one blunts an enemy’s blade, another loosens chains from the wrists, and so on. The Sigrdrífumál shows the same pattern in a smaller way, when the valkyrie Sigrdrífa teaches Sigurðr rune songs for sword, ale, and healing.

The voice is the teaching. No tune survives in the Poetic Edda. The Hávamál gives purposes and boasts, never notes on a staff. Any modern galdr with set tunes, robes, and staged circle work is a modern rebuilding, and it is honest when called so.

The Sigrdrífumál does give one short spoken prayer: “Hail to Day! Hail to the sons of Day! To Night and her daughter hail! With placid eyes behold us here, and here sitting give us victory. Hail to the Æsir! Hail to the Asyniur! Hail to the bounteous earth! Words and wisdom give to us noble twain, and healing hands while we live.” (Stanzas 3 and 4, translated by Benjamin Thorpe, 1866.) Learn it by heart and speak it aloud at the same window three mornings in a row.

Where this comes from: The charm list is in the Hávamál and the prayer is in the Sigrdrífumál, both in the Poetic Edda, in Benjamin Thorpe’s 1866 translation, now public domain. No tunes survive.

Óðinn’s songs

At the end of the Hávamál, Óðinn lists eighteen songs he knows and says what each one does. “For the third I know, if I have great need to restrain my foes, the weapons’ edge I deaden.” “For the fourth I know, if men place bonds on my limbs, I so sing that I can walk; the fetter starts from my feet, and the manacle from my hands.” (Stanzas 150 and 151, translated by Benjamin Thorpe, 1866.)

Other songs help against strife and cares, still a fire in a house, calm the wind and sea for a boat, end hatred among people, and keep warriors safe in battle. The list is a catalogue of needs, and each song answers one.

Óðinn does not give the words. No tune and no verse of any song survives. He does say something about giving: “’Tis better not to pray than too much offer; a gift ever looks to a return.” (Stanza 147.) Know what you need before you speak, and do not give more than you can spare.

The Merseburg charms

The Merseburg charms are two short spoken charms in Old High German, the old language of southern Germany. They were copied into a church book in the 900s, and they are among the very few pagan Germanic charms to survive in verse. Both open with a short story, then give a command.

In the first, women called idisi sit down. Some bind a captive, some hold back an army, and some pick at the fetters. The charm ends with the words: “Leap out of the bonds, escape the enemies.”

In the second, Wodan, the southern form of Óðinn, and other gods ride into a wood, and Balder’s foal sprains its foot. Wodan, who knew the charms well, sets it right: “bone to bone, blood to blood, limb to limb, as if they were glued.”

Notice the shape: a story about gods or spirits, then the words that copy what they did. That is the old pattern for a spoken charm. This book offers no cure, and if you are ill you should see a clinician.

Where this comes from: The Merseburg charms are in a church manuscript from the 900s, in Old High German. The summaries and quoted lines here are in plain English.

Mound-neighbors

Low green mounds in the fields are old burial places. Haugr, the burial mound, stands in Icelandic sagas as the home of the dead. In some sagas the dead keep watch over cattle and weather, and gifts are laid and words spoken at mounds. The Sigrdrífumál tells the living to care for the dead: “Let a mound be raised for those departed; let their hands and head be washed, combed, and wiped dry.” (Stanza 34, translated by Benjamin Thorpe, 1866.)

The Hávamál says what lasts: “Cattle die, kindred die, we ourselves also die; but the fair fame never dies of him who has earned it.” (Stanza 76.) Mounds and ship-settings, stone outlines shaped like ships, are found by archaeologists, and you can visit them. The talking dead come from Icelandic sagas, which is a story pattern, not a map.

The memorial toast

The Old Norse remembered the dead with a feast. After a death, kin gathered for the funeral feast, called the erfi. In Ynglinga saga, an heir did not take his father’s seat until he had drunk the bragarfull, a special cup, and made a vow. Then he sat in the high seat. In Hákonar saga góða, toasts were drunk to the memory of departed kin. Such a toast was called a minni.

Keep the shape at your table. Serve a plain, hot meal, including a dish they used to make. Before the meal, each person names the dead in turn, with one true sentence. Then raise a cup and drink to their memory. That is the minni. As in the old feast, follow the toast with a vow: one thing you will do in their honor.

Once a year is plenty, at Yule or Winter Nights. If it is your family’s custom, set one extra plate, a later folk habit. Leave a little food out for birds the next morning. Steady remembrance matters more than grandeur. Tears are fine.

Where this comes from: The funeral feast, the bragarfull, and the minni toast are in Ynglinga saga and Hákonar saga góða in Snorri’s Heimskringla. The extra plate is later folk custom.

Hávamál: friends and gifts

The Hávamál has a great deal to say about friendship, and it is practical. “Long is and indirect the way to a bad friend’s, though by the road he dwell; but to a good friend’s the paths lie direct, though he be far away.” (Stanza 34, translated by Benjamin Thorpe, 1866.)

Friends trade gifts and visits. “With arms and vestments friends should each other gladden… Givers and requiters are longest friends, if all [else] goes well.” (Stanza 41.) “If thou hast a friend whom thou fully trustest… thou shouldst blend thy mind with his, and gifts exchange, and often go to see him.” (Stanza 44.) The poem is also honest about friends you do not trust: be pleasant, but guard your thoughts.

This is advice from a world with no safety net, where a friend meant a roof, a meal, and an ally at the assembly. Give one friend a small gift this month, and visit them. The old rule is that gifts are returned and visits are repaid.

Where this comes from: Hávamál stanzas 34 to 46 in the Poetic Edda, in Benjamin Thorpe’s 1866 translation, now public domain.

Some of her counsel, about enemies and women, belongs to its own time. This book keeps the parts that still help: keep faith with your kin, keep your oaths, keep your head, and care for the dead.
