Working with the Old Ways

Rest your tired bones and troubled mind. This book shares the old Norse ways, which were about keeping good company with the living, the dead, and the land. It is meant to bring you comfort and a sense of connection with your ancestors. If parts of it do not fit your life or beliefs, leave them behind and keep what helps. I have tried to present these old ways as accurately as the sources allow, and I mark each claim with where it comes from. Use the lines on each page to write, reflect, and build your own practice in whatever way works best for you.

The Poems

The Poems Behind This Book

This book leans on a handful of old texts, and you will meet their names on almost every page. Here is what they are. The Poetic Edda is a collection of about thirty poems about gods and heroes. Most of them survive in one Icelandic manuscript, the Codex Regius, copied around 1270. Their poets are unknown. The poems were probably composed between about 900 and 1200 and kept by memory first, so their dates are best guesses.

The Prose Edda was written by Snorri Sturluson, an Icelandic chieftain and poet, around 1220. He wrote it as a handbook for young poets, retelling the myths in plain prose so they could follow the old verse. Snorri was a Christian writing two centuries after Iceland converted, so his version is useful, tidy, and late. Where he and the poems disagree, this book says so.

Two more kinds of text appear here. Icelandic sagas are prose stories about families and kings, written down in the 1200s and later. The medieval rune poems are short verses, one for each rune. The next pages give a short guide to the poems you will meet most often. You do not need to read them first. Come back to this guide whenever a title puzzles you.

Where this comes from: The Codex Regius dates to about 1270 and Snorri’s Prose Edda to about 1220. The dates of the individual poems are debated.

The Lokasenna: Loki’s quarrel

The Lokasenna is a flyting, a contest of insults, and it is the poem this book cites most when it speaks of Loki. The sea-giant Ægir (Aegir) brews ale for the gods and holds a feast. Þórr is away in the east. Loki has already killed one of Ægir’s servants and been chased out, but he walks back in and demands a seat.

One by one he insults the gods, accusing them of cowardice and unfaithfulness. They answer him, and he has a cruel reply for each. At the end Þórr arrives and threatens him with his hammer, and Loki leaves. Two stanzas show the poem’s edge. First Loki reminds Óðinn of their bond: “Odin! dost thou remember when we in early days blended our blood together?” Then Þórr, on arriving: “Silence, thou impure being! My mighty hammer, Miollnir, shall stop thy prating. I will thy head from thy neck strike; then will thy life be ended.” (Stanzas 9 and 57, translated by Benjamin Thorpe, 1866.)

A short prose ending in the manuscript tells what follows. The gods catch Loki, who had hidden as a salmon, and bind him with the entrails of his son. Skaði hangs a serpent over his face. His wife, Sigyn, holds a bowl to catch the venom. When she turns away to empty it, the venom drips on him, and his struggling shakes the earth.

Where this comes from: The Lokasenna and its prose ending are in the Poetic Edda. The excerpts are Benjamin Thorpe’s 1866 translation, now public domain.

Who Were the Old Norse

The Old Norse were the people of Scandinavia and the North Atlantic, mostly from about 750 to 1050 CE, a time called the Viking Age. They were not one kingdom. They lived on farms, islands, and fjords in what is now Norway, Denmark, Sweden, and Iceland, and the sea was their main road. They spoke Old Norse, a North Germanic language, and cut it in runes, letters carved into stone and wood.

We know them through two kinds of record. Stones and graves show what people left behind: names, burials, tools, and ships. Books tell the stories, and they were written down later, mostly in Iceland. The Poetic Edda is a collection of old poems about gods and heroes. Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda retells the myths in plain prose. Icelandic sagas are prose stories about families and kings.

Stones keep what people finished, and stories keep what people cared about. You need both. On each page I will tell you which kind of record I am using.

Where this comes from: Runestones and the Viking Age dates come from archaeology. The Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda were copied down centuries later.

Who Were the Old Norse

No Viking wrote a handbook of belief for us to find. Most of what we know comes from people who were not Vikings, or who lived long after. The Roman writer Tacitus described Germanic peoples around 98 CE, from outside, for a Roman audience. The Arab traveler Ibn Fadlan watched a chieftain’s funeral on the Volga River in 922 and wrote it down as a stranger. Icelandic Christians copied the old poems and sagas onto vellum, a prepared animal skin, in the 1200s. They saved the voices, but they changed the frame.

That means the record has gaps, and this book says so. It is about the Old Norse only. It is not Celtic lore with Norse names, and it is not a modern mix pretending to be old. When modern people have filled a gap, I say so plainly, and I name the source of everything else. Every lore page ends with a note called “Where this comes from.”

Ask who wrote each source and why, and you will know how far to trust it.

Where this comes from: How this book works. Its method is modern, but it is built on old sources.

Nine Realms

Creation: fire meets mist

Before anything had a name, there was a gap. The Old Norse called it Ginnungagap, probably “the yawning gap.” It was not a person but a place: a huge empty space with fire on one side and ice on the other. Múspell (Muspell), the fire world, burned at one edge, guarded by the giant Surtr (Surt) with a flaming sword. Niflheimr (Niflheim), the mist world, lay at the other, where poisonous rivers froze into frost.

Then fire and ice met. Sparks from Múspell crossed the gap and melted the frost into drops, and the drops became Ymir, the first giant. A cow named Auðumla (Audumla) fed on the salty ice until a man named Búri (Buri) appeared. He was the grandfather of Óðinn (Odin). Óðinn and his brothers killed Ymir and made the world from his body: earth from his flesh, sea from his blood, sky from his skull, mountains from his bones, and trees from his hair.

People came last. In Snorri Sturluson’s Gylfaginning, three gods found two fallen trees on a shore, Ask and Embla, ash and elm. The gods gave them breath, wit, and warmth, and the trees stood up as the first people. You can take a small version of this home. When you bake bread or mend a seam, you give care to plain material.

Where this comes from: The creation story is from the Völuspá (Voluspa), Vafþrúðnismál (Vafthrudnismal), and Grímnismál (Grimnismal) in the Poetic Edda, and from Snorri’s Prose Edda. The household use was written for this book.

Nine Realms

Nine Realms: sky, middle, and wild

The Old Norse told of nine realms, or worlds, held together by one great tree, Yggdrasill (Yggdrasil). They were not stacked like floors. Ásgarðr (Asgard) is the high home of the Æsir (Aesir) gods, such as Óðinn and Þórr, reached by the rainbow bridge Bifröst (Bifrost). Miðgarðr (Midgard) is the middle world where people live. Jörmungandr (Jormungandr), the world serpent, coils in the sea around its edge.

Vanaheimr (Vanaheim) is the home of the Vanir, an older family of gods tied to harvest and plenty, such as Njörðr (Njord), Freyr, and Freyja. Jötunheimr (Jotunheim) is the wild land of the jötnar (giants), who stand for chaos and untamed nature. The Æsir and the giants are often at odds.

Neither the Poetic Edda nor Snorri’s Prose Edda gives one map of the nine realms. The ring-shaped diagram you may have seen is a modern way to draw them. Use the realms to check your week. The Æsir bring oath and order. The Vanir bring harvest and plenty. The giants bring the wild edges you cannot fully control. Which one does your week live in most, and which one is it missing?

Where this comes from: The realms and Bifröst are named in the Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda. The ring-shaped map was drawn for this book.

Nine Realms: light, dark, fire, ice, and the dead

Álfheimr (Alfheim) is the bright home of the light elves. Svartálfaheimr (Svartalfheim), also called Niðavellir (Nidavellir) in some sources, is the underground home of the dwarfs and dark elves, whose smiths forged the gods’ finest treasures.

Múspell is the world of fire, ruled by the giant Surtr. Niflheimr (Niflheim) is the world of ice and fog. At its center is the spring Hvergelmir, and one root of Yggdrasill reaches down to it. The two worlds stand on opposite sides of the gap where the world was made.

Hel is the world of the dead, ruled by a giantess also named Hel. In Snorri’s Prose Edda she receives those who die of old age or sickness, not in battle. This book does not describe the road there. The practice is simpler: keep the names of your dead in use, as you do on the Ancestors pages.

Where this comes from: These realms are named in the Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda. The name Niðavellir and the sorting of elves into light and dark come from later sources.

Nine Realms

Yggdrasill: the ash that holds

Yggdrasill is a giant ash tree that holds the nine worlds together. Its three roots reach three wells: Hvergelmir in Niflheimr, the well of Mímir (Mimir), and the well of Urðr (Urd), where the Nornir (Norns) sit and shape fate. A stag feeds on its shoots above. The squirrel Ratatoskr (Ratatosk) runs up and down the trunk carrying messages, and he scolds both ends as he goes.

Read the tree as a picture of how things connect, not as a road map. No source describes a home ritual for it, so this book does not invent one. What you can take home is simple. Everything depends on something else. Roots need water. News travels the trunk like gossip. What you feed at the roots shows up later in the leaves.

Give your roots steady care, such as sleep, regular meals, and honest talk. Notice which roots are dry this week, and water one of them on purpose.

Where this comes from: The tree and its tenants are described in the Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda.

Nine Realms

Where the dead go: Valhöll (Valhalla), Fólkvangr (Folkvangr), Hel

The dead do not all go to one place. Valhöll (Valhalla) is Óðinn’s hall, roofed with shields and raftered with spears, and it takes half of those slain in battle. Freyja takes the other half to Fólkvangr (Folkvangr), her own field. Hel keeps those who die of sickness or old age in her fenced hall, which is neither burning nor shining. A grim shore is set aside for oath-breakers and murderers. Baldr went down to Hel, and he stayed there after the ransom failed.

Valhöll and Fólkvangr appear in the Poetic Edda. Hel’s hall appears in Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda and in the Poetic Edda, both written down late. Burial mounds tell a plainer story. They hold grave goods, ship settings, and feasting gear, which show care for the journey. Stories and mound earth are two kinds of witness. Neither cancels the other, and you can hold both while you grieve.

Your memory of someone is a form of care. It can last longer than any set of beliefs. You do not need the right belief to honor them. You need to keep their name in use. Speak it, write it, and keep one thing they loved in your home.

Where this comes from: Valhöll, Fólkvangr, and Hel are described in the Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda, both copied down late. The mounds are known from archaeology.

Nine Realms

Ragnarok: winter without spring

Ragnarök (Ragnarok) is the end of the world, told in the Völuspá. First comes Fimbulwinter, three winters in a row with no summer between. Families break their promises to one another, and an age of axes begins. The sun darkens and the stars fall. Naglfar, a ship made of dead men’s nails, floats free. Heimdallr (Heimdall) blows his horn, and the gods ride out to the plain of Vígríðr (Vigrid).

There they fight. Óðinn falls to the wolf Fenrir. Þórr (Thor) kills the world serpent and falls dead nine steps later. Loki and Heimdallr kill each other. Fire burns the world.

But the poem does not stop there. Earth rises green from the sea a second time. Baldr and Höðr (Hodr) return, and Víðarr (Vidar) and Váli (Vali) live on. The golden game pieces the gods once played with are found again in the grass. When something in your life ends, look for what grows after it. Name the ending, then find one small sign of new growth. You can grieve the loss and still look for it.

Where this comes from: The doom and the greening are from the Völuspá, retold in Snorri’s Prose Edda. This book offers no rites for the end of the world.

Grimoire prompt
Name one ending in your life. Name what grew after it, even something small. Give it three dated lines and read them aloud once.

Nine Realms

Doors: mounds and edges

In many old stories you cross into another world at an edge, not at a center. In Icelandic stories, burial mounds are said to stand open at Yule, the midwinter feast, and the dead walk near the living. A mound, a shoreline, and a feast night are all places where one world touches another.

Treat this as a story pattern, not a literal gate. Mounds mark crossing points in Icelandic sagas, and the Yule opening comes mostly from later Icelandic folk belief. Some farms still leave certain hills and landing places alone. That is folk custom, related to the stories but separate from them. Your own street can feel different at dusk. That feeling is real, but it is your own, not proof about other worlds.

Use the pattern to pay attention. Go out at dusk and notice the edges near you: a gate, a water line, a crossing. Write down what changes at each one.

Where this comes from: Mounds as doors appear in Icelandic sagas. The idea that they open at Yule comes from later Icelandic folk belief.

Guest rules: Hávamál manners

The Hávamál is full of house rules for guests and hosts. It tells a traveler to look around before he sits down, to speak little, and to listen long. It tells him never to mock a guest or a host. In the old songs, oath-breakers lose their good name.

These rules make sense as household habits first and as scholarship second. Folk custom keeps related rules in Yule peace and threshold greetings, which are related but not the same. Today, turn the rules around. You are the host in your own home, and your home tells visitors what to expect.

Your own table can follow one rule: no guest should leave hungry. Make that a habit.

Where this comes from: The guest rules are from the Hávamál.

Crooked time: promises across winter

The Old Norse did not always tell a story in a straight line. The Völuspá starts at the beginning of the world, jumps forward into doom, then past it into green earth rising again. Their year worked the same way. Icelanders counted years in winters, and oaths sworn at Yule were meant to hold through the summer, so a vow spoken on a winter night came due in spring.

Your own year works this way too. School terms and work rotas set the pace, and anniversaries arrive before you feel ready. A promise made in winter comes due in spring.

Pick one promise you made this winter and check it against the spring. Ask whether it still stands, and what it asks of you now. A written promise is easier to keep than a remembered one. Put a reminder on your calendar for the first warm day of spring.

Where this comes from: The looping style is in the Völuspá and Icelandic sagas, and winter-counting is in the sagas.

Neither-times: dawn and dusk

Dawn and dusk, when the light is neither day nor night, show up in many old stories. In Icelandic sagas, people meet at the shore, where land meets water. Dreams carry warnings and guide both voyages and killings. Some families still keep a Yule-night watch and draw water at dawn.

Dream warnings appear often in Icelandic sagas, but they are a story device, not proof that anyone kept dream books. This book does not decode dream symbols.

The practice is a short watch at dawn or dusk. Stand at one window or step outside for five minutes, and notice the light and the sounds. Noticing the change between day and night slows you down and trains your attention.

Where this comes from: Dream warnings appear as a repeated device in Icelandic sagas. The dawn-and-dusk watch was written for this book.

Rǫgn: Gods

Æsir and Vanir: The God Tribes

Rǫgn is an old Norse word for the powers: the gods and the forces that rule the world. The gods come in two families. The Æsir include Óðinn, Þórr, and Frigg. They live in Ásgarðr, the high realm in the sky, and they care about oath, battle, and order. The Vanir include Njörðr, Freyr, and Freyja. They live in Vanaheimr and care about the sea, the harvest, and plenty. Giants and elves live in other realms and belong to neither family.

The two families once fought a war and then made peace. The next page tells how it ended. Scholars think the story may remember two peoples who later joined together. It comes to us from the Völuspá, the seeress’s prophecy in the Poetic Edda, and from Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda, written around 1220. Both were written down centuries after the Viking Age, so the details are uncertain.

Think of the two families as two households that learned to share one valley. Each keeps its own job. The Æsir keep oath and order, and the Vanir keep work and plenty. A home that has only one of them is missing something.

Where this comes from: The Æsir and Vanir are in the Völuspá (Poetic Edda) and Snorri’s Prose Edda, both copied late. The idea that they were once two peoples is a scholars’ theory, not proven.

Grimoire prompt

The Æsir–Vanir war

The Æsir and Vanir once fought a war. The Völuspá says the Æsir broke a truce, and the Vanir fought back. Neither side could win for good, so they made peace.

The peace was built on hostages, a person from each side sent to live with the other. Njörðr and Freyr went to live among the Æsir. Hænir (Hoenir) and Mímir (Mimir) went to the Vanir. A hostage was a guest, fed and treated well, and that gave both sides a reason to keep their word. A promise is easy to break. A person sitting at the other table is harder to forget.

Njörðr and Freyr spent time in Ásgarðr as part of the treaty, but their home is Vanaheimr. The stories do not describe life after the treaty, so think of it this way: each family has its own home and its own job, and they meet at the table. The Völuspá and Snorri’s Prose Edda tell this as medieval Icelandic story, not as history of a real war.

Where this comes from: The war and the hostage exchange are told in the Völuspá and Snorri’s Prose Edda, both copied late.

Gods of Ásgarðr

Óðinn: the one-eyed

Óðinn (Odin) is the chief of the Æsir, the high one, and the god of wisdom, poetry, war, and the dead. He rides the eight-legged horse Sleipnir, shown on picture stones and thin gold medallions called bracteates. Two ravens, Huginn and Muninn, fly out each day and bring him news: thought and memory. Two wolves, Geri and Freki, follow at his heels. The Eddas also call him Alföðr (Allfather).

He paid a high price for wisdom. He gave one eye to the well of Mímir (Mimir) for a drink of its water. He hung nine nights on Yggdrasill, wounded by his own spear, to win the runes. The Hávamál gives his long counsel on wits and measure. The Poetic Edda shows him generous and terrible by turns, and he breaks oaths too.

Later books make him a kindly Allfather for modern worship, which is a modern softening. Jackson Crawford, a modern scholar of Old Norse, has argued that the name Alföðr may mean ‘All Order’ rather than ‘All-Father’. This reading is debated, and this book does not settle it.

The lesson is in the price. Óðinn wants to know things, and that hunger never comes free. Steady attention counts for more than an altar nobody visits. Give a set hour each week to learning a real craft, a poem, or a history.

Where this comes from: Óðinn’s eye, hanging, ravens, wolves, and horse are in the Poetic Edda, Snorri’s Prose Edda, and on stones. The ‘All Order’ reading is Jackson Crawford’s, and it is debated.

Days of the week

The Romans invented our seven-day week and named each day for a god or heavenly body: the Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, and Saturn. In the first centuries CE, Germanic peoples, the ancestors of the Norse and English, began using it.

They kept the week but not the Roman gods. For each Roman god they chose the Germanic god who seemed closest.

Sunday · sunnudagr
Sól, the sun goddess, who drives the sun across the sky.

Monday · mánadagr
Máni, the moon god and Sól’s brother, who drives the moon across the sky.

Tuesday · týsdagr
Týr, god of oaths and law. He was also a god of battle and victory, so he was matched with Rome’s Mars, god of war.

Wednesday · óðinsdagr
Óðinn, the high one. He wandered in disguise, ruled poetry and speech, and received the dead, so he was matched with Rome’s Mercury, god of speech.

Thursday · þórsdagr
Þórr, the thunderer. He ruled thunder and carried the hammer Mjölnir, so he was matched with Rome’s Jupiter, who wielded the thunderbolt.

Friday · frjádagr
Frigg, Óðinn’s wife and goddess of marriage, so she was matched with Rome’s Venus, goddess of love. Some scholars think Freyja.

Saturday · laugardagr
The Norse named it for bathing, not a god. Laug means “bath,” so laugardagr is “washing day.” English kept Rome’s Saturn.

Where this comes from: The Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda.

Vitki

Vitki: Rune-Reader

A vitki is a person who works with runes. The old word means something close to “wise one.” The clearest picture comes from Óðinn himself in the Hávamál. He hangs nine nights in a wind-tossed tree, hungry and thirsty, until he sees the runes below and seizes them with a shout. Then he carves them into wood and reddens the cuts with color.

In the Sigrdrífumál, the valkyrie Sigrdrífa, whom Völsunga saga calls Brynhildr, teaches the hero Sigurðr the same craft. She puts victory runes on the sword hilt, ale runes on the drinking horn, and birth runes on the palm. Knowing here is handwork: you cut, you color, and you speak it aloud.

The medieval rune poems (Norwegian, Icelandic, and Anglo-Saxon) are the alphabet book behind the craft. They hold one short verse for each rune letter, each tied to a plain thing: wealth must move, ice bears no weight, the sun steadies. What they never show is anyone laying runes out to tell fortunes. That is modern and should be called so.

Learn one rune letter a week from the rune poems. Cut or draw it on a wood scrap or card, and speak its verse aloud. Then set it to one honest household use, such as scoring the loaf before baking, marking a tool handle, or chalking a mark above the door. Hands learn what guessing cannot.

Where this comes from: The hanging is in the Hávamál, the counsel in the Sigrdrífumál, and the verses in the rune poems, with a parallel in Völsunga saga. Rune-spread divination is modern.

Elves

Bright elves and dark elves

Elves (álfar) are shy neighbors in the old poems. You mostly glimpse them at the edge of lamplight. The Grímnismál names Álfheimr as their home. The poems often pair them with the gods in the phrase “Æsir and elves,” and the Lokasenna seats them at Ægir’s feast. They are beautiful and dangerous outsiders, tied to mounds and fields, and they are neither angels nor demons.

Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda splits them into two groups: light-elves, bright as the sun to look at, and dark-elves, black as pitch, who live down in the earth. That split is late and tidy. The Poetic Edda names elves often but never draws the line cleanly. Some scholars think Snorri may be sorting dwarfs under another name, calling them the smiths of Svartálfaheimr, the dark-elf world. Treat the boundary as blurred.

Think of the elves as unseen neighbors who watch from the edge. You can honor them by stepping softly, keeping paths clear, and leaving one quiet place alone. Practice this in one corner of your week, not in all of it.

Where this comes from: Álfheimr is in Grímnismál, and the light and dark split is in Snorri’s Prose Edda, which is late and tidy.

Álfablót (Alfablot): the closed door

Álfablót (alfablot) means “elf sacrifice.” It was an autumn rite, and almost everything we know comes from one traveler. Sigvatr Þórðarson (Thordarson), a skald, or court poet, of King Óláfr (Olaf), went from farm to farm in Norway seeking a bed and was turned away. In his poem Austrfararvísur (Austrfararvisur), he says each farm was holding an álfablót, which was holy and closed to strangers. The doors were barred and no guest was taken in.

That closed door is nearly all outsiders saw of the rite. Sigvatr’s poem is from the early eleventh century, written at about the time. It describes being shut out, not the ritual itself. No prayer, altar plan, or list of offerings survives. The sources support only a few plain points: autumn, closed doors, and family only. Later farm custom leaves food and drink at mounds and keeps certain hills alone, but that is folk custom and not the same rite.

You can keep the same spirit at home. Pick one evening, close the door to noise, eat quietly, and speak one thank-you aloud. This is one skald’s account of farms, not a rule for every farm.

Where this comes from: The álfablót is in Sigvatr’s Austrfararvísur, a poem from the time. No account of the rite itself survives.

Dwarfs

Born from earth and sea

Dwarfs (dvergar) are the smiths and craftsmen of the old stories, and they live in the earth and rock. The Völuspá gives them the longest list of names in the Poetic Edda but almost no description. After the gods shape the world, the dwarfs come to life in the earth, like maggots that grow in flesh, according to Snorri’s Prose Edda. In the Völuspá they are shaped from Ymir’s bones and from the blood of the sea-giant Brimir, and the gods give them mind and shape.

The long list holds names like Northri and Southri, Austri and Vestri, Gandalf and Thorin. Many of the names point to stone, staff, or advice. Think of dwarfs first as powers of the earth, and only second as people. They avoid daylight and work metal. Later books make them short, comic miners with axes, and that picture is modern. The Poetic Edda shows skill, advice, and cost, and no comedy.

Four of their names match the four directions: Northri, Southri, Austri, and Vestri. Stand in your home and face each direction in turn. Name the wall or corner that faces each one. Knowing a place well helps you move through it with care.

Treasures from the forge

The gods could not have their finest tools without dwarfs to make them, and every gift had a cost. In Snorri Sturluson’s Skáldskaparmál (Skaldskaparmal), Loki cut the hair of Sif, Þórr’s wife, for sport, and had to pay for it. He got new hair of gold from the dwarfs called the sons of Ívaldi (Ivaldi), who also made Skíðblaðnir, a ship that folds up, and Gungnir, a spear that never misses. Then Loki bet his head against the dwarfs Brokkr and Sindri that they could not do better. They made Draupnir, a gold ring that drips more gold, Gullinbursti, a golden boar, and Mjölnir, a hammer with a short handle.

Loki’s head was on the line, and only his wordplay saved him. The hammer’s handle is short because Loki, as a fly, stung the smith’s brow while he worked. The cost matters more than the shine. Every treasure costs something, good work costs effort, and careless tricks with other people’s goods bring trouble.

Sif’s gold hair shows the pattern: a careless wrong is repaired by fine work, and the repair is paid for. Use that lesson in your own life. Fine work deserves to be honored, not just owned.

Völundr (Volund) the smith

The Rök stone sidebar

A tall grey stone stands in Östergötland, in Sweden. It is covered edge to edge with runes. It is the Rök stone, raised in the 800s. It holds the longest runic text from the Viking Age known to us. Vetrnætr (Vetrnaetr): kin and burial mounds

Sigrblót: turn to summer

Sigrblót (Sigrblot) means “victory sacrifice,” an offering made at the start of summer for success. The kings’ sagas, such as Ynglinga saga in Snorri’s Heimskringla, name it as a spring offering. Runes

Runes cut on stone

Run your finger along a stone’s cuts in low light and you feel the shape before you read it. A rune is a letter of the old runic alphabet. The Elder row, the older set in order, has twenty-four runes. They are cut on spearheads, on gold bracteates (thin gold medallions), and on standing stones, from roughly the second to the eighth century. The row begins fehu-uruz-þurisaz-ansuz-raidō-kenaz. Those six runes give the row its name, futhark, which is taken from its first six letters. The Kylver stone on Gotland carries the whole Elder row in order. Centuries later, the Younger row uses sixteen signs, in short-twig and long-branch forms. It was cut from roughly the ninth to the twelfth century on stones such as Rök and Jelling.

The two rows differ in length, not in purpose. The Elder row spells things out fully. Its twenty-four runes are grouped into three families of eight. Later books call them the kin of Freyr, Hagall, and Týr. The Younger row shortens the writing to sixteen signs and leaves more to the reader. Stones give family names and memories. They say a stone was raised for a father. They never give spells or fortunes. The medieval rune poems (Norwegian, Icelandic, and Anglo-Saxon) pair each rune with a short saying. Wealth must move. Ice gives no grip. A torch shows the way. Read each saying slowly, one at a time.

Your hands can learn the runes before your mind does. The first cuts were made by hands holding chisels, and hands still learn them best. Draw straight lines with a ruler, say each name aloud, and count the branches on each rune. Using your hand and your voice together helps the shapes stick. If the whole row feels like too much, start with the first six runes.

Where this comes from: The rows are cut on stones from the 2nd to the 12th century, and the rune poems give the verses. The study practice is modern.

It is not a Viking Age sign.

They stand as early modern folk practice, not Iron Age practice. In books such as Galdrabók, their power comes from calling on Christ, Mary, and angels alongside older names. Galdr: the sung charm

A charm is a short set of words said or sung for a purpose. A galdr is a charm that is sung or spoken aloud, not one thought in silence. In the Hávamál, Óðinn lists the songs he knows, the way a carpenter lists tools, and each one has its work named: one blunts an enemy’s blade, another loosens chains from the wrists, and so on. The Sigrdrífumál shows the same pattern in a smaller way, when Brynhildr teaches Sigurðr rune songs for sword, ale, and healing.

The voice is the teaching. No tune survives in the Poetic Edda. The Hávamál gives purposes and boasts, never notes on a staff, and the medieval rune poems give names and meanings, never a music book. Any modern galdr with set tunes, robes, and staged circle work is a modern rebuilding, and it is honest when called so. What the sources show is plainer: a verse learned by heart, said aloud, and said with care.

Keep it simple. Learn one verse by heart and speak it aloud in a low voice at the same window three mornings in a row, without strain. A sung charm here means practiced words that calm your nerves, not weather you command.

Circling and cleansing

Walking a ring around a person or place, with your feet and your voice, is an old form of protection. The walk is called circling. The turn runs sunwise, meaning clockwise in the northern world, across northern custom. You walk around the person or place with a blessing until the circle closes. The ring says this ground is watched. The cleansing says this body is dressed for the day. A hallway, a garden bed, and a bedside all suit the ring. Your own words work well before sleep.
