Working with the Old Ways

Rest your tired bones and troubled mind. This book shares the old Norse ways, which were about keeping good company with the living, the dead, and the land. It is meant to bring you comfort and a sense of connection with your ancestors. If parts of it do not fit your life or beliefs, leave them behind and keep what helps. I have tried to present these old ways as accurately as the sources allow, and I mark each claim with where it comes from. Use the prompt at the end of each page, and the lined pages, to write, reflect, and build your own practice in whatever way works best for you. Write your answers on a lined page or in a notebook of your own, and date them.

This book mentions plants from folk custom and from medieval medical books, but it does not replace a doctor. Never eat, drink, or apply any plant from these pages based on this book alone. If you forage, go with an experienced guide, check each plant against at least three trusted sources, and take only what you need, leaving most of it behind. For medicinal herbs, I buy from Mountain Rose Herbs: https://mountainroseherbs.com/

If a source is late, or scholars disagree, the note says so. Anything described as modern or rebuilt was written for your home and is not an old practice. The main sources are the Poetic Edda, Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda, the medieval rune poems, and the sagas listed in Resources.

How to say the names

Old Norse names look hard, but a few rules cover most of them. The letter Þ (þ) sounds like “th” in thin. The letter Ð (ð) sounds like “th” in this. An accent mark over a vowel means hold it a little longer. The letter ǫ sounds like the “aw” in law, and j sounds like English “y.” The final -r on names like Baldr and Heimdallr is a grammar ending, said roughly like “ur” (BAHL-dur, HAIM-dahl-ur). English spellings usually drop it, which is why you will also see Balder and Heimdall.

The Poems

The Poems Behind This Book

This book leans on a handful of old texts, and you will meet their names on almost every page. Here is what they are. The Poetic Edda is a collection of about thirty old Icelandic poems about gods and heroes, such as Óðinn, Þórr, and Loki. Most of this book’s quotes and stories come from it. Nobody knows who wrote the poems.

The Prose Edda was written by Snorri Sturluson, an Icelandic poet. He wrote it to preserve the style of the old poetry, retelling the myths in plain prose so that poets could keep using it. Many people believe his Christian beliefs influenced his writing, and that may be true to some extent. Still, scholars widely agree that he was genuinely preserving the old Norse stories, and they treat his work as a valuable resource for understanding the past.

You will also see two other kinds of writing. Icelandic sagas are long prose stories about real families and kings. The rune poems are short verses that each describe one rune. The next pages list the poems you will meet most often, so you can look them up whenever a title puzzles you.

Where this comes from: The Poetic Edda and Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda, the two collections described on this page.

The Poems

Poems of wisdom and the world

Vǫluspá (Voluspa), “the seeress’s prophecy.” A seeress tells Óðinn the story of the world, from its making to its ending at Ragnarǫk and its return.

Hávamál (Havamal), “Sayings of the High One.” Óðinn’s advice on guests, friends, and keeping your wits. It also tells how he won the runes and lists the charms he knows.

Grímnismál (Grimnismal), “Grímnir’s sayings.” Óðinn, in disguise and held captive, names the halls of the gods and the shape of the worlds.

Vafþrúðnismál (Vafthrudnismal), “Vafþrúðnir’s sayings.” Óðinn and a wise giant test each other with questions about the world, its making, and its end.

Sigrdrífumál (Sigrdrifumal), “Sigrdrífa’s sayings.” A valkyrie wakes and teaches the hero Sigurðr runes and counsel.

The rune poems. Three medieval poems, Norwegian, Icelandic, and Anglo-Saxon, give each rune a short verse of its own.

Where this comes from: These poems are in the Poetic Edda and the medieval rune poems.

The Old Norse

Who Were the Old Norse

The Old Norse were the people of Scandinavia and the North Atlantic, mostly from about 750 to 1050 CE, a time called the Viking Age. They were not one kingdom. They lived on farms, islands, and fjords in what is now Norway, Denmark, Sweden, and Iceland, and the sea was their main road. They spoke a language we call Old Norse. They wrote it with runes, their own alphabet, which they carved into stone and wood. It is called “Old” because it is the ancestor of Icelandic, Norwegian, Swedish, and Danish. It is now a dead language, meaning no one grows up speaking it, though specialists still study it and some can speak it. Icelandic has changed the least.

We learn about them from two kinds of evidence. The first is what they left behind: runestones, graves, tools, and ships. The second is books. Their stories were not written down until long after the Viking Age, mostly in Iceland. The earlier pages on The Poems introduce the main ones.

What they left behind shows how people actually lived. The stories show what they believed and cared about. Combining the two gives the most well-rounded view of the past.

Where this comes from: Runestones and the Viking Age dates come from archaeology. The Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda were copied down centuries later.

The Old Norse

Limitations

No Viking wrote a handbook of belief for us to find. Most of what we know comes from people who were not Vikings, or who lived long after. The Roman writer Tacitus described Germanic peoples around 98 CE, from outside, for a Roman audience. The Arab traveler Ibn Fadlan watched a chieftain’s funeral on the Volga River in 922 and wrote it down as a stranger. Icelandic Christians later wrote down the old poems and sagas. They preserved the old stories, though their own beliefs may have shaped how they told them.

Even so, these sources are valuable. Scholars have studied them closely for generations, and together they give a rich picture of Norse myth, belief, and daily life. This book is built on the Old Norse sources themselves.

The Old Norse

Who kept the knowledge

The old knowledge was not held evenly. Different people kept different parts. The vǫlva (seeress) traveled between farms, was fed and honored, and was asked about the coming season, as Eiríks saga rauða shows. The vitki (rune-master) carved and read runes, as Egill does in Egils saga. The skald (poet) kept memory in verse, and Óðinn’s mead of poetry was said to be the source of that gift. The goði (a local leader, in Iceland) kept the gatherings and the law. Households kept the daily customs, who was fed, who was named, and who was remembered.

Each of these has pages in this book. Notice that most of them were learned from someone, by being near them and watching. That is true of most family knowledge too: recipes, songs, names, repairs, and stories.

Where this comes from: Eiríks saga rauða for the vǫlva, Egils saga and the Sigrdrífumál for runes, and the Prose Edda for the skald and the mead. The goði appears in the Icelandic sagas.

Nine Realms

Creation: fire meets mist

Before anything had a name, there was a gap. The Old Norse called it Ginnungagap, probably “the yawning gap.” It was a huge empty space with a world at each end. Múspell (Muspell), the fire world, lay in the south, guarded by the giant Surtr (Surt) with a flaming sword. Niflheimr (Niflheim), the world of mist and cold, lay in the north. Poisonous rivers ran out of Niflheimr, and far from their source they froze into ice.

At first the ice only piled up in the north of the gap, layer on layer. Then sparks and hot air blew north from Múspell, and where the heat touched the ice it melted into drops. From those drops came Ymir, the first giant. Next came a cow named Auðumla (Audumla), who licked the salty ice. Over three days her licking freed a man from it, and his name was Búri (Buri). Later Búri’s grandsons, Óðinn (Odin) and his two brothers, killed Ymir and made the world from his body: earth from his flesh, sea from his blood, sky from his skull, mountains from his bones, and trees from his hair.

People came last. According to the Vǫluspá (Voluspa), three gods, Óðinn (Odin), Hœnir (Hoenir), and Lóðurr (Lodur), found two fallen trees on a shore, an ash and an elm. The ash became the first man, Ask, and the elm became the first woman, Embla. Óðinn gave them breath, Hœnir gave them wit, and Lóðurr gave them warmth, and they stood up as the first people.

Where this comes from: The creation story is from the Vǫluspá (Voluspa), Vafþrúðnismál (Vafthrudnismal), and Grímnismál (Grimnismal) in the Poetic Edda, and from Snorri’s Prose Edda.

Nine Realms

The nine realms at a glance

The Old Norse told of nine realms, or worlds, held together by one great tree, Yggdrasill (Yggdrasil). No single poem lists all nine in one place, so this page gathers them from the Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda. The ring-shaped map you may have seen is modern, because the sources give no map.

Ásgarðr (Asgard): home of the Æsir gods, high in the sky, reached by the rainbow bridge Bifrǫst.
Vanaheimr (Vanaheim): home of the Vanir, tied to harvest and plenty.
Álfheimr (Alfheim): home of the light elves, given to Freyr.
Miðgarðr (Midgard): the middle world, where people live.
Jǫtunheimr (Jotunheim): the wild land of the giants.
Svartálfaheimr (Svartalfheim): the underground home of the dwarfs and dark elves, also called Niðavellir in some sources.
Múspell (Muspell): the world of fire, ruled by the giant Surtr.
Niflheimr (Niflheim): the world of ice and fog, with the spring Hvergelmir.
Hel: the world of the dead, ruled by a giantess also named Hel.

Where this comes from: The realms are named across the Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda. Niðavellir is in the Vǫluspá, and Svartálfaheimr is in Snorri’s Gylfaginning. The order of this list is this book’s.

Nine Realms

Yggdrasill: the ash that holds

Yggdrasill is a giant ash tree that holds the nine worlds together. Its three roots reach three wells: Hvergelmir in Niflheimr, the well of Mímir (Mimir), and the well of Urðr (Urd), where the Nornir (Norns) sit and shape fate. A stag feeds on its shoots above. The squirrel Ratatoskr (Ratatosk) runs up and down the trunk carrying messages, and he scolds both ends as he goes.

Yggdrasill is most likely the tree Óðinn hung on when he sacrificed himself for the runes. It has other names. Mjǫtviðr (Mjotvidr) means fate-tree. In the Vǫluspá the seeress remembers it from before it grew, still under the earth, perhaps a seed. Hlautviðr (Hlautvidr) means tree of sacrificial blood. Mímameiðr (Mimameidr) means Mímir’s tree, and Mímir’s well of wisdom lies at the foot of one of its roots. The Fjǫlsvinnsmál names it.

Read the tree as a picture of how things connect, not as a road map. No source describes a home ritual for it, so this book does not invent one. What you can take home is simple. Everything depends on something else. Roots need water. News travels the trunk like gossip. What you feed at the roots shows up later in the leaves.

Give your roots steady care, such as sleep, regular meals, and honest talk. Notice which roots are dry this week, and water one of them on purpose.

Where this comes from: The tree and its tenants are described in the Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda. The other names come from the Vǫluspá and the Fjǫlsvinnsmál, as taught by Dr. Jackson Crawford.

Nine Realms

Where the dead go: Valhǫll (Valhalla), Fólkvangr (Folkvangr), Hel

The dead did not all go to one place. Here is where each place sits.

Ásgarðr (Asgard), in the sky: Valhǫll (Valhalla) is Óðinn’s hall, roofed with shields and raftered with spears. It takes half of those slain in battle, who feast there as the einherjar until Ragnarǫk. Freyja takes the other half to Fólkvangr (Folkvangr), her own field. The poems do not name its realm. Most retellings put it in Ásgarðr, since Freyja lives among the gods.
Hel, in the north and below: Hel’s hall, Éljúðnir, keeps those who die of sickness or old age. The road to it runs down and north, over the river Gjǫll, and the hall lies under one root of Yggdrasill. Baldr went there and stayed. Snorri puts Hel inside Niflheimr, so lists differ on whether they are one world or two.
Nástrǫnd, in the cold north of Hel: the Corpse Shore, a hall with its doors to the north and venom dripping through the roof. The Vǫluspá places oath-breakers and murderers there. Snorri adds that the worst of the wicked go on from Hel to Niflhel, deeper in Niflheimr.
Miðgarðr (Midgard), the middle world: some of the dead stayed near their families. Sagas tell of the dead living on inside their burial mounds, and inside Helgafell, a holy mountain in Iceland. The drowned were said to go to Rán, the sea goddess, in her hall under the waves.

Your memory of someone is a form of care, and it can last longer than any set of beliefs. Keep their name in use. Speak it, write it, and keep one thing they loved in your home.

Where this comes from: Valhǫll, Fólkvangr, Hel, and Nástrǫnd are in the Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda, both copied down late. Snorri is the one who places Hel in Niflheimr. Helgafell and the living dead in mounds are in the Icelandic sagas, and the mounds themselves are known from archaeology.

Nine Realms

Ragnarǫk: winter without spring

Ragnarǫk (Ragnarok) is the end of the world, told in the Vǫluspá. First comes Fimbulwinter, three winters in a row with no summer between. Families break their promises to one another, and an age of axes begins. The sun darkens and the stars fall. Naglfar, a ship made of dead men’s nails, floats free. Heimdallr (Heimdall) blows his horn, and the gods ride out to the plain of Vígríðr (Vigrid).

The poem also says a wolf swallows the moon and spatters the sky and air with blood, that the sun loses its shine, and that the winds blow wild. Set beside eclipses, the lines fit well. In a lunar eclipse the moon turns red and shadowed. In a total solar eclipse the sun loses its light, and the wind and weather can shift as the air cools. Some readers also take the two wolves, Skǫll and Hati, for the moon’s north and south nodes, the two points where an eclipse can happen. That is a modern reading.

There they fight. Óðinn falls to the wolf Fenrir. Þórr (Thor) kills the world serpent and falls dead nine steps later. Loki and Heimdallr kill each other. Fire burns the world.

But the poem does not stop there. Earth rises green from the sea a second time. Baldr and Hǫðr (Hodr) return, and Víðarr (Vidar) and Váli (Vali) live on. The golden game pieces the gods once played with are found again in the grass. When something in your life ends, look for what grows after it. Name the ending, then find one small sign of new growth. You can grieve the loss and still look for it.

Where this comes from: The doom and the greening are from the Vǫluspá, retold in Snorri’s Prose Edda. This book offers no rites for the end of the world.

Rǫgn: Gods

Æsir and Vanir: The God Tribes

Rǫgn is an old Norse word for the powers: the gods and the forces that rule the world. The gods come in two families. The Æsir include Óðinn, Þórr, and Frigg. They live in Ásgarðr, the high realm in the sky, and they care about oath, battle, and order. The Vanir include Njǫrðr, Freyr, and Freyja. They live in Vanaheimr and care about the sea, the harvest, and plenty. Giants and elves live in other realms and belong to neither family.

The two families once fought a war and then made peace. The next page tells how it ended. Scholars think the story may remember two peoples who later joined together. It comes to us from the Vǫluspá, the seeress’s prophecy in the Poetic Edda, and from Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda, written around 1220. Both were written down centuries after the Viking Age, so the details are uncertain.

Think of the two families as two households that learned to share one valley. Each keeps its own job. The Æsir keep oath and order, and the Vanir keep work and plenty. A home that has only one of them is missing something.

Where this comes from: The Æsir and Vanir are in the Vǫluspá (Poetic Edda) and Snorri’s Prose Edda, both copied late. The idea that they were once two peoples is a scholars’ theory, not proven.

Grimoire prompt

Gods

The Æsir–Vanir war

The Æsir and Vanir once fought a war. The Vǫluspá says the Æsir broke a truce, and the Vanir fought back. Neither side could win for good, so they made peace.

The peace was built on hostages, a person from each side sent to live with the other. Njǫrðr and Freyr went to live among the Æsir. Hænir (Hoenir) and Mímir (Mimir) went to the Vanir. A hostage was a guest, fed and treated well, and that gave both sides a reason to keep their word. A promise is easy to break. A person sitting at the other table is harder to forget.

Njǫrðr and Freyr spent time in Ásgarðr as part of the treaty, but their home is Vanaheimr. The stories do not describe life after the treaty, so think of it this way: each family has its own home and its own job, and they meet at the table. The Vǫluspá and Snorri’s Prose Edda tell this as medieval Icelandic story, not as history of a real war.

Where this comes from: The war and the hostage exchange are told in the Vǫluspá and Snorri’s Prose Edda, both copied late.

Gods of Ásgarðr

Óðinn: the one-eyed

Óðinn (Odin) is the chief of the Æsir, the high one, and the god of wisdom, poetry, war, and the dead. He rides the eight-legged horse Sleipnir, shown on picture stones and thin gold medallions called bracteates. Two ravens, Huginn and Muninn, fly out each day and bring him news: thought and memory. Two wolves, Geri and Freki, follow at his heels. The Eddas also call him Alfǫðr (Allfather).

He paid a high price for wisdom. He gave one eye to the well of Mímir (Mimir) for a drink of its water. He hung nine nights on Yggdrasill, wounded by his own spear, to win the runes. The Hávamál gives his long counsel on wits and measure. The Poetic Edda shows him generous and terrible by turns, and he breaks oaths too.

Later books make him a kindly Allfather for modern worship, which is a modern softening. Jackson Crawford, a modern scholar of Old Norse, has argued that the name Alfǫðr may mean ‘All Order’ rather than ‘All-Father’. This reading is debated, and this book does not settle it.

The lesson is in the price. Óðinn wants to know things, and that hunger never comes free. Steady attention counts for more than an altar nobody visits. Give a set hour each week to learning a real craft, a poem, or a history.

Where this comes from: Óðinn’s eye, hanging, ravens, wolves, and horse are in the Poetic Edda, Snorri’s Prose Edda, and on stones. The ‘All Order’ reading is Jackson Crawford’s, and it is debated.

Gods of Ásgarðr

Óðinn: names, mead, and the hall

Óðinn has many names. In the Grímnismál he says he is called Grímnir, Hroptr, Yggr, Gagnráðr, and more. The name Yggdrasill is usually read as “Yggr’s horse,” and Yggr is Óðinn, because he hung on the tree like a rider on a horse.

His best-known theft is the mead of poetry. In Snorri’s Skáldskaparmál, dwarfs killed the wise man Kvasir and brewed his blood into mead, and a giant, Suttungr, ended up hiding it in a mountain under his daughter Gunnlǫð’s guard. Óðinn, in disguise, tricked his way in, spent three nights with Gunnlǫð, drank all the mead, and flew out as an eagle. Whoever drinks it becomes a poet. The Hávamál remembers it: “Oblivion’s heron… With this bird’s pinions I was fettered in Gunnlod’s dwelling.” (Stanza 13, translated by Benjamin Thorpe, 1866.)

Valhǫll is his hall. The einherjar, warriors chosen by the valkyries, feast and fight there each day until Ragnarǫk, when Óðinn falls to the wolf Fenrir. That is the other half of his story: he gathers warriors, and he knows what he is gathering them for.

Where this comes from: The names are in the Grímnismál. The mead of poetry is in Snorri’s Skáldskaparmál and the Hávamál. Valhǫll and the einherjar are in the Grímnismál and Snorri’s Gylfaginning.

Gods of Ásgarðr

Frigg: wife and seer

Frigg is the wife of Óðinn and the mother of Baldr. She sits beside the high seat and sees further than she tells. She knows what will come and speaks of it to almost no one. She spins and weaves and keeps the keys and inner doors of the household.

Two stories show her. In the Lokasenna, a poem in the Poetic Edda, she warns Loki that Baldr would have made him pay for his insults, and Loki answers that he is the reason Baldr never rides home. In Snorri’s Prose Edda, when Baldr dreams of his death, she makes everything in the world swear not to harm him. She works by foresight and preparation.

Some scholars think Frigg and Freyja may be two names for one goddess. Here Frigg holds marriage and household foresight, and Freyja holds love and growth. In your home, Frigg stands for planning ahead. Look at what winter will ask of your home: lamp oil, savings, rest. Lay one thing ready this week, and cool quarrels before they catch fire.

Where this comes from: Frigg’s foresight is in the Poetic Edda, and her oath-taking for Baldr is in Snorri’s Prose Edda. Scholars debate whether she and Freyja were once one goddess.

Freyja of Fólkvangr

Skaði: winter's bride

Loki: The Trickster

No devil stands here, whatever later sermons made of him. Snorri’s Gylfaginning says Loki was born a giant, the son of Fárbauti and Laufey, and the old texts sometimes name him for his mother, Laufey’s son. He lives among the Æsir in Ásgarðr, and in the Lokasenna he reminds Óðinn that the two swore blood-brotherhood. With the giantess Angrboða he fathered Hel, the wolf Fenrir, and the world serpent.

His mischief starts small and turns costly. In Snorri’s Prose Edda he cuts off the golden hair of Sif, the wife of Þórr, and Þórr threatens to break every bone in him. Loki goes to the dwarfs for new hair, and the gifts he brings back include Þórr’s hammer.

The harm grows. He arranges the death of Baldr, and in the Lokasenna he insults every god at a feast until Þórr threatens him with his hammer and he leaves. At last the gods bind him in a cave with a serpent’s venom dripping on him. At Ragnarǫk he breaks free. He is clever, cruel, and useful.

He is cleverness without loyalty, and he cannot be flattered into favors. If trouble with him comes near, bring repair and nothing else. Own the mess and repair it fully, facing the person you hurt directly. The binding and the insults are told in the Poetic Edda and Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda.

List your tangles. Strike the ones that are only noise. Keep the one that is truly a mess to mend. Clarity is the only answer he respects. One cleanly repaired tangle is worth more than a year of excuses.

Týr (Tyr) of the oath

Týr (Tyr) is the god of oaths and law, and a god of battle and victory. He is best known for losing his right hand. The gods wanted to bind the wolf Fenrir, Loki’s son, who had grown too strong. Fenrir would let them tie him only if one of them put a hand in his mouth as a pledge. Týr did, and when the chain held and Fenrir could not break free, the wolf bit the hand off.

The story is told in Snorri’s Prose Edda, and the lost hand is named in the Lokasenna. The Sigrdrífumál and the medieval rune poems (Norwegian, Icelandic, and Anglo-Saxon) name his rune for victory. Þórr guards by strength, and Týr guards by oath.

Days of the week

The Romans invented our seven-day week and named each day for a god or heavenly body: the Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, and Saturn. In the first centuries CE, Germanic peoples, the ancestors of the Norse and English, began using it.

They kept the week but not the Roman gods. For each Roman god they chose the Germanic god who seemed closest.

Sunday · sunnudagr
Sól, the sun goddess, who drives the sun across the sky.

Monday · mánadagr
Máni, the moon god and Sól’s brother, who drives the moon across the sky.

Tuesday · týsdagr
Týr, god of oaths and law. He was also a god of battle and victory, so he was matched with Rome’s Mars, god of war.

Wednesday · óðinsdagr
Óðinn, the high one. He wandered in disguise, ruled poetry and speech, and received the dead, so he was matched with Rome’s Mercury, god of speech.

Thursday · þórsdagr
Þórr, the thunderer. He ruled thunder and carried the hammer Mjǫllnir, so he was matched with Rome’s Jupiter, who wielded the thunderbolt.

Friday · frjádagr
Frigg, Óðinn’s wife and goddess of marriage, so she was matched with Rome’s Venus, goddess of love. Some scholars say it was Freyja instead.

Saturday · laugardagr
The Norse named it for bathing, not a god. Laug means “bath,” so laugardagr is “washing day.” English kept Rome’s Saturn.

Where this comes from: The Poetic Edda and Snorri’s Prose Edda.

Landvættir (Landvaettir): land-spirits

Landvættir (landvaettir) are land spirits, the unseen guardians of a place. Icelandic stories and law treat them as real neighbors who must be respected. The Landnámabók, a book of Iceland’s first settlers, says ships had to remove the carved heads from their prows before landing, so the spirits would not be frightened.

Egils saga shows how seriously people took them. The hero Egill raises a scorn pole, a post topped with a horse’s head, and calls on the land spirits to drive out the king who wronged him.

The message is plain for any household: you are a guest on the land. Ask leave, keep your landing clean, and do not foul water or wood. Well-kept ground stays kinder than fouled ground, season after season.

The four guardians of Iceland

In Snorri’s Heimskringla, in the saga of Óláfr Tryggvason, a king of Denmark wants to invade Iceland and sends a sorcerer ahead in the shape of a whale. Each of them drives the sorcerer back, and the king gives up the invasion. The four land spirits appear on the Icelandic coat of arms today.

The landvættir are not gods. They guard a place the way a household guards a farm. They are the unseen owners of the land, and the old sources treat them as neighbors to be respected.

Nornir: The Norns

The Nornir (Norns) are the women who shape fate. The Vǫluspá names three: Urðr (Urd), Verðandi (Verdandi), and Skuld. Their names are often read as what has been, what is becoming, and what must be.

The death of Baldr

Baldr dreams of his own death, and the gods take the dream seriously. His mother, Frigg, goes through the world and takes an oath from fire and water, iron and all metals, stones, earth, trees, sicknesses, beasts, birds, poison, and snakes that none will harm Baldr. Hermóðr (Hermod) rides to Hel and asks for Baldr back. Hel says he may return if everything in the world weeps for him. All do, except one giantess, Þǫkk (Thokk), who says she will weep only dry tears. She is usually understood to be Loki in disguise, and Baldr stays in Hel. The gods later bind Loki for his part in it, as the Loki page tells.

Vǫlva: staff-bearer

A vǫlva (volva) is a seeress, a woman who carries a staff and sees what is hidden. The Vǫluspá is a vǫlva speaking. She is called to the hall of Óðinn and paid to remember. Hamingja

Hamingja: Your Luck

Hamingja is personal luck and family luck, and in Icelandic sagas it follows a person like a second skin. Men speak of luck as plainly as they speak of ships. It grows through generosity and oath-keeping and shrinks through stinginess and broken oaths. Good luck settles on the generous, and bad luck follows the oath breaker.

Every treasure costs something, good work costs effort, and careless tricks with other people’s goods bring trouble.

Sif’s gold hair shows the pattern: a careless wrong is repaired by fine work, and the repair is paid for. Use that lesson in your own life. Fine work deserves to be honored, not just owned.

Yule

Jól (Yule): midwinter peace

Jól (Yule) is the old northern midwinter feast, most likely held just after the winter solstice. The word is plural because the celebration lasted about three nights, with feasting and drinking. Horse was one of the meats served. Doors were often barred early. Toasts went to gods and ancestors, and a peace lay over households, so that even enemies could eat without feud.

Jól was also the time for oaths. The sagas tell of oaths sworn then, and an oath sworn at Jól was iron-bound and held extra sacred. Breaking it could be punished by death. People might swear over a boar, the way one swears on a Bible, or over a ring. Some poems and sagas tell of vows made at Jól being kept later in battle.

Folk custom keeps the same shape in plainer clothes. Straw, a candle, and a cleaned house mark the season. The home grows quiet as the old names are spoken. You do not need a crowd or costumes.

Jól is the time for vows. Before you make a new one, look back at the vows you made last winter and say honestly which you kept. Then speak the names of the dead over supper, one sentence each.

Dísablót

Dísablót: the ladies’ feast

Dísablót took place during Winter Nights (Vetrnætr), the three nights in late October that open winter. The name means a sacrifice to the dísir (disir), female guardian spirits linked to kin, households, and fate. The offering was made in the Dísarsalr (Disarsalr), the hall of the dísir. It was a private rite, held by a family for its own dísir, who may be a form of that family’s ancestors. Some sources link Freyja to them, since she is also called Vanadís, lady of the Vanir. The Icelandic sagas name the feast but never describe it in detail. A later Swedish market, Disting, is sometimes linked to this feast, and the Idisi in the Merseburg charms were women called on to help warriors.

The sources are thin, and this page says so. No prayer, altar plan, or menu survives, and the Disting fair is a late link, not proof. This book builds one honest practice, clearly modern: a Winter Nights supper that honors the women whose work shaped your household, living and dead, by name and aloud. Naming them gives them a place at the table.

Once a year is enough. Cook one dish from stored food, and name each woman in one true sentence while you eat. The fuller supper plan is on the Ancestors pages. Naming women who are often forgotten keeps their memory alive in your family.

Sigrblót: turn to summer

Midsummer: light that does not leave

Midsummer is a summer gathering around a bonfire and fresh green branches, and people in the north still remember it. It comes with flower crowns and hill fires. Many northern farms kept some part of it. Folk collectors such as Tillhagen recorded Midsummer greens and the custom of picking herbs at noon, and fires lit against a night that barely gets dark. Yarrow: greni

Yarrow is easy to recognize once you learn its signs. Its leaves are feathery, like green lace. Its flat white flower heads stand along hedges all summer. Old books call it soldier’s woundwort. In Old Icelandic medical books it is greni, also written millefolium, “thousand leaves.”

A fifteenth-century Icelandic medical manuscript, AM 434 a 12mo, gives yarrow three uses: chew the root while fasting for toothache; drink the juice with wine to clear the urine; and crush it and lay it on a wound, which it says heals every wound remarkably well. The same manuscript also uses yarrow in a ritual to find a thief, mixing the herb with a carved sign, a spoken charm, and a Christian closing formula. These are quoted here as history, not as instructions.

This book asks you only to look. Yarrow can cause skin irritation in some people. Never use it in place of prescribed medicine, and never for deep or doubtful wounds. Count ten clumps on a walk and sketch one leaf.

Wells and the gifts left in them

Bog water and spring water have kept things for a very long time. Long ago, people laid swords, cauldrons, and brooches in lakes, bogs, and springs. Diggers still find them where rivers slow and springs rise. Scholars call these gifts votive deposits, objects given on purpose to a place. In the Vǫluspá, even Óðinn gives to water: he pledged an eye to the well of Mímir for a drink of wisdom. What mattered most was the giving itself.

A well is a place with its own history. It is not a machine that grants wishes. Old deposits are well documented: they were found, lifted from the ground, and recorded. Tossing coins into a living spring is a newer habit, and coins can pollute the water. Never drink wild water, because you cannot know what is in it.

When you visit, go as a guest of the place. Ask leave before you lift any water, and sit a while to listen before you leave. Take nothing away from the site, and leave nothing behind that will not rot or wash clean. Keep any promise you made to the place. The visit takes under an hour.

Dawn, dusk, and Yule night

Twice a day, the light is neither fully day nor fully night. Once a year, at Yule (midwinter), the season does something similar. In Icelandic folk belief, the mounds stood open on Yule night and the dead could walk near the living. Dawn and dusk carry a smaller daily version of that opening. The house is quiet, the edges show clearly, and birds wake or settle as the light shifts. Even a city window shows the change.

In old stories, edges were meeting times. People meet at the shore, where land meets water. Bargains were made and messages passed, and dreams carried warnings that guided voyages and killings. Dream warnings are a story device in the sagas, not proof that anyone kept dream books, and this book does not decode dreams. The phrase “thin places” for such spots is modern. Showing up on time at the edge was the offering.

The old practice for keeping an edge is útiseta, sitting out alone at dusk or dawn, described earlier in this book. Take it in the same small and safe way: three dawns or three dusks in a row, ten minutes each, at a window or on a step. Notice the light and count three sounds. If cloud hides the sun, keep the same hour anyway.

Runes

What runes are

Runes are the letters the early Germanic peoples carved in wood, stone, bone, and metal. The oldest inscriptions date from about the second century CE. The alphabet is called the futhark, named for its first six letters: f, u, th, a, r, k. It came in three main forms. The Elder Futhark has 24 runes and was used from about the 2nd to the 8th century. The Younger Futhark has 16 and was used in Viking Age Scandinavia, from about the 9th to the 12th century. The Anglo-Saxon futhorc grew to as many as 33 and was used in England.

Each rune has a name, a sound, and a meaning from the rune poems. Fehu is the sound f and also the word for cattle and wealth. On some inscriptions a single rune stands for the whole word it names, as scholars think the l-rune stands for laukaz, “leek.”

Vitki: Rune-Reader

A vitki is a person who works with runes. The old word means something close to “wise one.” The clearest picture comes from Óðinn himself in the Hávamál. He hangs nine nights in a wind-tossed tree, hungry and thirsty, until he sees the runes below and seizes them with a shout. Then he carves them into wood and reddens the cuts with color.

In the Sigrdrífumál, the valkyrie Sigrdrífa, whom Vǫlsunga saga calls Brynhildr, teaches the hero Sigurðr the same craft. She puts victory runes on the sword hilt, ale runes on the drinking horn, and birth runes on the palm. Knowing here is handwork: you cut, you color, and you speak it aloud.

The medieval rune poems (Norwegian, Icelandic, and Anglo-Saxon) are the alphabet book behind the craft. They hold one short verse for each rune letter, each tied to a plain thing: wealth must move, ice bears no weight, the sun steadies. What they never show is anyone laying runes out to tell fortunes. That is modern and should be called so.

Learn one rune letter a week from the rune poems, and say its verse aloud. The pages that follow give the rows, the names, and how to carve.

Writing in runes

To write in runes, spell by sound, not by spelling. The Elder Futhark has no capitals and no rules for spaces. Inscriptions mark the end of a word with a dot or two, and may run left to right or right to left.

ᚨaᛒbᛞdᛖeᚠfᚷgᚺhᛁiᛃjᚲkᛚlᛗmᚾnᛟoᛈpᚱrᛊsᛏtᚢuᚹwᛉzᚦthᛜngᛇei

Carving runes

The old sources say how runes were made. Sigrdrífa tells Sigurðr to carve them on the thing they are for: a sword’s hilt, a horn, the palm, a ship’s prow. In Egils saga, Egill carves runes and then reddens them with blood. The Hávamál asks the same questions in stanza 146: do you know how to carve a rune, how to read it, how to color it, and how to test it?

To make a simple carved rune, you need a smooth, dry piece of wood and a sharp knife, used carefully and always cutting away from your hand. Draw the runes in pencil first. Cut each stroke across the grain, with two cuts that meet in a V. Then fill the cuts with red ink or paint. This book does not ask for blood.

Egill’s rule is the one that matters most: do not carve runes you cannot read. Before you cut a rune, know its name and the sound or word it stands for.

A bindrune joins two or more runes by sharing one stave, the vertical line. Bindrunes appear on many early and Viking Age inscriptions, to save space or to make a name look like one sign.

Runes

Casting lots

The Roman writer Tacitus, writing about 98 CE, describes how Germanic peoples took omens. They cut a branch from a fruit tree into small slips and cut a mark on each one. The slips were scattered on a white cloth. A priest, or in a private matter the father of the household, prayed to the gods, looked up at the sky, and lifted three slips one at a time. He read them by the marks. If the lots forbade a plan, it was dropped for that day.

Tacitus says marks, not runes, so we cannot say these were runes. What this shows is that drawing marked slips for a yes-or-no answer was an old habit. Rune casting as it is practiced now, with named meanings and layouts, is modern.

To try the old shape, gather ten or more small pieces of wood from fallen twigs, and cut a rune you know on each. Lay out a light cloth. Ask a question about something you can actually do. Pull three slips, one at a time, without looking. Read each by its name and its poem line. Then decide what you will do. The runes give you something to think with, not an order.

Where this comes from: Tacitus, Germania, chapter 10. The runes, the question, and the reading method are modern.

Galdr: the sung charm

A charm is a short set of words said or sung for a purpose. A galdr is a charm that is sung or spoken aloud, not one thought in silence. In the Hávamál, Óðinn lists the songs he knows, the way a carpenter lists tools, and each one has its work named: one blunts an enemy’s blade, another loosens chains from the wrists, and so on. The Sigrdrífumál shows the same pattern in a smaller way, when the valkyrie Sigrdrífa teaches Sigurðr rune songs for sword, ale, and healing.

The voice is the teaching. No tune survives in the Poetic Edda. The Hávamál gives purposes and boasts, never notes on a staff. Any modern galdr with set tunes, robes, and staged circle work is a modern rebuilding, and it is honest when called so.

The Sigrdrífumál does give one short spoken prayer: “Hail to Day! Hail to the sons of Day! To Night and her daughter hail! With placid eyes behold us here, and here sitting give us victory. Hail to the Æsir! Hail to the Asyniur! Hail to the bounteous earth! Words and wisdom give to us noble twain, and healing hands while we live.” (Stanzas 3 and 4, translated by Benjamin Thorpe, 1866.) Learn it by heart and speak it aloud at the same window three mornings in a row.

Níð: words as weapons

The old Norse feared words. A curse could be spoken, carved, or raised on a pole. The best-known case is in Egils saga. Low green mounds in the fields are old burial places. Haugr, the burial mound, stands in Icelandic sagas as the home of the dead. Waking the dead

The old poems know the dead can be woken. In the Hervararkviða, Hervǫr sails to an island of burial mounds. At night, with fire burning over the mounds, she stands before her father Angantýr’s mound and calls him up. She asks for the sword that lies buried with him. He warns her away, and she does not back down. At last he gives it to her.

In the Grógaldr, a young man named Svipdagr stands at his dead mother Gróa’s mound and asks her to wake and sing him charms for a hard road. She sings nine of them.

Both scenes follow one pattern: go to the mound, call the dead by name, and ask for one clear thing. In both, the dead may warn you or refuse, and the living need nerve and a good reason.

A modern way to follow the pattern does not need a graveyard or darkness. Sit with a grave, a photograph, or something that belonged to the person. Say their name aloud. Ask for one thing: advice, strength, or a story. Then be quiet and listen. Thank them, and leave something small. The dead may not answer in words.

Where this comes from: The Hervararkviða is in Hervarar saga ok Heiðreks. The Grógaldr is the first poem of the Svipdagsmál, kept in later paper manuscripts. The practice above is modern.

The memorial toast

The Old Norse remembered the dead with a feast. After a death, kin gathered for the funeral feast, called the erfi. In Ynglinga saga, an heir did not take his father’s seat until he had drunk the bragarfull, a special cup, and made a vow. Then he sat in the high seat. In Hákonar saga góða, toasts were drunk to the memory of departed kin. Such a toast was called a minni.

Keep the shape at your table. Serve a plain, hot meal, including a dish they used to make. Before the meal, each person names the dead in turn, with one true sentence. Then raise a cup and drink to their memory. That is the minni. As in the old feast, follow the toast with a vow: one thing you will do in their honor.

Once a year is plenty, at Yule or Winter Nights. If it is your family’s custom, set one extra plate, a later folk habit. Leave a little food out for birds the next morning. Steady remembrance matters more than grandeur. Tears are fine.

Where this comes from: The funeral feast, the bragarfull, and the minni toast are in Ynglinga saga and Hákonar saga góða in Snorri’s Heimskringla. The extra plate is later folk custom.

Hávamál: friends and gifts

The Hávamál has a great deal to say about friendship, and it is practical. “Long is and indirect the way to a bad friend’s, though by the road he dwell; but to a good friend’s the paths lie direct, though he be far away.” (Stanza 34, translated by Benjamin Thorpe, 1866.)

Friends trade gifts and visits. “With arms and vestments friends should each other gladden… Givers and requiters are longest friends, if all [else] goes well.” (Stanza 41.) “If thou hast a friend whom thou fully trustest… thou shouldst blend thy mind with his, and gifts exchange, and often go to see him.” (Stanza 44.) The poem is also honest about friends you do not trust: be pleasant, but guard your thoughts.

This is advice from a world with no safety net, where a friend meant a roof, a meal, and an ally at the assembly. Give one friend a small gift this month, and visit them. The old rule is that gifts are returned and visits are repaid.

Where this comes from: Hávamál stanzas 34 to 46 in the Poetic Edda, in Benjamin Thorpe’s 1866 translation, now public domain.

Sigrdrífa’s counsel

In the Sigrdrífumál, the valkyrie Sigrdrífa gives Sigurðr eleven pieces of counsel before he rides on. She numbers them: this I counsel first, this I counsel secondly, and so on. They are the nearest thing the Poetic Edda has to a code for living.

Among them: be blameless toward your kin, and take no hasty vengeance (the first). Swear no oath that is not true, for “cruel bonds follow broken faith: accursed is the faith-breaker” (the second). Do not argue with a fool in the assembly (the third). Do not quarrel when drunk, for “wine steals the wits of many” (the sixth). Bury the dead you find, and raise them a mound (the ninth). Do not trust a promise from the kin of a man you have wronged (the tenth). (Stanzas 22 to 35, translated by Benjamin Thorpe, 1866.)

Some of her counsel, about enemies and women, belongs to its own time. This book keeps the parts that still help: keep faith with your kin, keep your oaths, keep your head, and care for the dead.

Go on reading

Four old texts go further into this subject. Read small parts in public translations before you buy anything. The Poetic Edda in Larrington’s translation opens with the seeress and the counsel of Hávamál. Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda in Faulkes’s version tells the stories plainly. The Vǫlsunga Saga in Byock’s translation follows Sigurðr to the pyre. Egils saga tells of a poem that buys back Egill’s own head in York. Borrow them first, so you can judge them fairly.

Read these books first as stories, and second as lessons. They are original old texts, in translation, not summaries. They reward slow reading. For each part, ask what happened, what it means at home, and what small act answers it. Let the answer settle over a week. A chapter a week is a good pace. Keep a notebook beside you, and write your answers there.

Start with one book and one starting day. The next morning, sit in the same spot and read the next section. An hour or less per sitting is plenty. Pick a time that suits you and keep it. When you finish, pass the book on, and mark the page that held you longest. Keeping to your reading plan is a small vow you can keep.
