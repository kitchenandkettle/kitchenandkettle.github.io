The Old Names

Before calendars, people named moons after what was happening on the ground. The Worm Moon meant thawing soil and returning robins. The Strawberry Moon meant it was time to pick. The Harvest Moon meant work late into the night by its light.

These names come from the Algonquin people of the northeastern United States, passed along by farmers and settlers who adopted them because they made sense. Each name tells you what the world is doing that month.

How to Use This Journal

Each full moon gets one page with the story behind its name and a prompt for reflection. Then four cycle pages — 🌑 New Moon, 🌒 Waxing, 🌕 Full, 🌘 Waning — give you space to track your own rhythm through that lunar cycle. At the end of each season, a seasonal recap page helps you step back and see the bigger pattern.

🌑 New Moon — Start. Set intentions. Plant seeds.

🌒 Waxing Moon — Build. Take action. Move forward.

🌕 Full Moon — Reflect. Notice what came together. Celebrate.

🌘 Waning Moon — Release. Let go. Clear space.

Honest note

This journal gives you a framework for checking in with yourself as the year turns. Use it however makes sense to you — agriculturally, spiritually, as a monthly reflection practice, or just because you like the moon names. There's no wrong way.

❄️ Snow Moon
February

The heaviest snow usually falls under this moon. Some tribes called it the Hunger Moon — the hardest month for hunting, stores running low, everyone watching the sky and waiting for the thaw that hasn't come yet.

What's running low? What do you need more of — not just things, but patience, energy, hope?

What are you ready to rest from? What can wait until the light comes back?

Winter Moons

Wolf · Snow · Cold

Winter moons rise early and stay long. The world is quiet. This is the season of rest — not laziness, but the real, deep kind where nothing is urgent and the ground is doing its own work underground. The garden is asleep. The kitchen runs on root vegetables and preserved things from fall. Candles get lit earlier.

🐺 Wolf Moon January — endurance, waiting, trusting what's dormant
❄️ Snow Moon February — hunger, scarcity, watching for the thaw
🌨️ Cold Moon December — rest, darkness, the long night

What's the first small sign that something is changing for you? What's softening?

Spring Moons

Worm · Pink · Flower

Spring moons feel urgent. Everything happens fast — the thaw, the bloom, the burst of green. The garden wakes up and immediately demands attention. The kitchen shifts from storage to fresh things. These moons are about starting, noticing, keeping up.

🪱 Worm Moon March — thaw, first signs, the ground softening
🌸 Pink Moon April — first flowers, what's showing up unasked
🌺 Flower Moon May — everything at once, the rush of growth
🍓 Strawberry Moon
June

Strawberries ripen. This moon marks the first real harvest of the year — the first thing you can eat straight off the plant, warm from the sun, no cooking required. A short window. If you don't pick them, they're gone.

What's ready to harvest in your life right now? What will pass if you don't catch it?

Summer Moons

Strawberry · Buck · Sturgeon

Summer moons are warm and late-rising. The days are long enough that the moon almost feels like an afterthought — until you're outside after dark and notice it hanging there, orange and huge near the horizon. The garden is producing faster than you can pick. The kitchen is full of bowls of tomatoes and peaches.

🍓 Strawberry Moon June — first harvest, catching what's ripe
🦌 Buck Moon July — full strength, too much, new growth
🐟 Sturgeon Moon August — gathering in, preserving, the work of abundance
🌽 Corn Moon
September

Corn is ready. Sometimes called the Harvest Moon when it falls closest to the equinox — the moon that rises right after sunset for several nights in a row, giving farmers extra light to bring in the crops. The year's work shows its results.

What's the harvest of your year so far? What did you plant (in any sense) that's come through?

🏹 Hunter's Moon
October

The fields are cleared, the animals are fattened for winter, and there's nothing left to hide behind. The Hunter's Moon rises bright after sunset, same as the Harvest Moon — extra light for the hunt. The world is stripping down.

What's been cleared away? What's visible now that wasn't before?

🦫 Beaver Moon
November

Beavers are building their winter dams, working fast before the freeze. This was also the moon to set beaver traps before the ponds iced over — securing warm furs for the winter ahead. Preparation, not panic.

What are you building to get through the winter? What shelter are you creating — for yourself or for someone else?

Autumn Moons

Corn · Hunter's · Beaver

Autumn moons are the best moons — bright, early-rising, the Harvest Moon giving you extra light for extra work. The garden is winding down. The kitchen smells like apples and squash and bread again. These moons are about gathering what you grew, clearing what's done, and getting ready.

🌽 Corn Moon September — the main harvest, what the year produced
🏹 Hunter's Moon October — stripping down, seeing clearly
🦫 Beaver Moon November — building shelter, preparing for winter

Go outside and look up

The moon will keep rising — same phases, same rhythm, same quiet pull on the tides and the soil and whatever draws you to look up at it. The journal is a way to mark time. To look back after a year and see what grew and what didn't and what you let go of without meaning to. However you use it — however the moons speak to you — that's the right way.
